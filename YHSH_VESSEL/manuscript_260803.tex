\begin{abstract}
{\color{violet}
Cooperative navigation among Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS) is challenging in partially observable maritime environments, where limited local sensing restricts situational awareness during multi-vessel encounters. This paper develops a communication-enabled cooperative navigation framework for coordinated path planning and collision avoidance. Each vessel encodes its local observation into a compact latent message, while messages from multiple neighboring vessels are aggregated with its own observation to extend situational awareness beyond the local sensing range. A COLREGs-conditioned Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture shares the perception module across encounter situations while retaining situation-specific decision functions. Communication is introduced after an initial local-navigation stage so that the policy first establishes stable navigation behavior before learning to exploit information exchanged among vessels.

The framework is evaluated in a GPU-batched maritime simulator with 16 simultaneously navigating vessels. Controlled experiments separately examine the necessity of communication, shared-perception expert design, multi-neighbor aggregation, message dimensionality, directional COLREGs shaping, and communication-introduction timing. Communication reduces collision rate while improving COLREGs compliance and navigation efficiency. The shared-perception MoE provides a favorable safety--model-size tradeoff relative to a single network and fully separated experts, and aggregating several spatially grounded messages is substantially safer than using only the nearest neighbor. Fleet-size, representative-trajectory, and latent-message analyses further examine the behavior of the learned cooperation, while heterogeneous-fleet and A* integration studies evaluate its use under mixed communication capability and route-level guidance. The results demonstrate that communication-enabled distributed intelligence can support cooperative and COLREGs-aware MASS navigation under partial observability.
}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
{\color{violet}
The rapid advances of autonomous navigation technologies have brought Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS) to the forefront of maritime research and regulation. Despite this progress, safe and efficient navigation in congested waters remains a major challenge to the practical deployment of MASS. Autonomous vessels need to avoid collisions while complying with the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs), particularly in dense maritime traffic where multiple vessels may interact simultaneously under uncertain and changing conditions \citep{Complexity,Sarhadi}.

Cooperative navigation involves multiple vessels coordinating their trajectories and sharing locally acquired information to improve navigational safety and efficiency \citep{Akdag,chen}. Each vessel observes only part of the surrounding environment and exchanges locally available information with neighboring vessels. The received information is combined with the vessel's own observation to determine its subsequent navigation behavior. Instead of relying on a complete global representation of the maritime environment, cooperative navigation distributes locally acquired information among vessels so that their trajectories can be coordinated according to surrounding traffic conditions.

Communication-enabled cooperative navigation involves three key operations. First, each vessel extracts navigation-relevant information from its local observation and encodes it into a compact message. Second, messages received from neighboring vessels within communication range are aggregated into a fixed-dimensional cooperative representation. Third, each vessel determines its trajectory by combining this representation with its own local observation. This information exchange extends situational awareness beyond individual sensing ranges and supports coordinated responses to potential conflicts. This communication distinguishes cooperative navigation from independent collision avoidance, where surrounding vessels are treated as moving obstacles and each vessel determines its trajectory solely from locally available observations.

Classical path-planning techniques include graph-search algorithms such as A* and Dijkstra's algorithm, sampling-based planners, and artificial potential field methods. Graph-search methods can determine optimal or near-optimal routes with respect to a predefined discretization and cost function, whereas local methods provide computationally efficient short-horizon guidance \citep{Yuan,58}. Model Predictive Control (MPC) can incorporate vessel dynamics, operational constraints, and short-term motion predictions into online trajectory planning, but its computational burden generally increases with the number of interacting vessels and the prediction horizon \citep{Sarhadi}. Rule-based methods explicitly encode COLREGs requirements, but their navigation behavior is largely determined by predefined encounter categories and can become difficult to specify for complicated multi-vessel configurations.

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) provides a learning-based approach to autonomous vessel navigation beyond predefined planning and encounter rules. Through repeated interactions with simulated maritime environments, an autonomous vessel can learn a collision-avoidance policy without explicitly programming every possible encounter and avoidance maneuver. Existing studies have shown that DRL can jointly account for goal reaching, collision avoidance, and COLREGs compliance within a learned navigation policy \citep{Zhao-COLREGs}. However, most single-agent DRL approaches model surrounding vessels as dynamic obstacles rather than cooperative agents. These vessels neither exchange information nor coordinate their trajectories, leaving each agent to navigate solely from observations available within its own sensing range.

Cooperative navigation introduces additional learning challenges in multi-agent environments. As the policy of each vessel evolves during training, the transition dynamics observed by one agent change with the policies of other agents, creating a non-stationary learning environment. Partial observability further limits the information available to each vessel. Radar, cameras, and other onboard sensors have finite ranges and fields of view, and each vessel therefore determines its navigation behavior using incomplete information about the surrounding maritime traffic \citep{Thombre}.

Inter-vessel communication can alleviate partial observability by exchanging information acquired locally by individual vessels \citep{Akdag,chen}. Direct transmission of raw observations, however, increases communication overhead and may convey information irrelevant to the current navigation task. Learned communication instead encodes the local observation of each vessel into a compact, task-relevant message optimized jointly with the navigation policy. The exchange of these messages supplements locally available observations with information acquired by neighboring vessels and establishes distributed situational awareness across the communicating vessels \citep{WangZhao2025}.

Communication alone, however, does not ensure effective cooperation. Coordinated navigation depends on the information encoded in each message, the aggregation of messages from a varying number of neighbors, and the use of the aggregated information in selecting navigation actions. These factors become increasingly important in dense maritime traffic, where several vessels may simultaneously enter communication range and create interacting collision risks. When a vessel receives messages from several neighbors simultaneously, direct concatenation makes the policy input depend on the number and ordering of those neighbors. A fixed-dimensional and permutation-invariant aggregation mechanism is therefore required for multi-vessel operation.

COLREGs compliance presents another challenge in multi-vessel environments. A vessel may simultaneously act as the give-way vessel relative to one neighbor and the stand-on vessel relative to another \citep{Complexity}. Treating each pairwise encounter independently without accounting for collision urgency can obscure the obligation associated with the most immediate conflict. Cooperative navigation in such encounters needs to account for both shared observations and the relative priority of concurrent COLREGs obligations.

Jointly learning goal-directed navigation, collision avoidance, and inter-vessel communication also increases the difficulty of policy optimization. When communication is active from the beginning of training, the control policy must learn basic navigation while simultaneously interpreting messages generated by an evolving MessageActor. This coupling can destabilize training before either representation becomes useful.

To address these challenges, a communication-enabled framework is developed for cooperative MASS navigation under partial observability. Each vessel encodes its local observation into a compact latent message and exchanges it with neighboring vessels. At the receiver, the message is combined with relative bearing and distance before aggregation so that the learned content remains associated with its physical origin. Messages from several nearby vessels are then converted into a fixed-dimensional representation and combined with the receiving vessel's local observation.

Multi-vessel COLREGs encounters are handled through a context-aware MoE architecture. The dominant encounter is first determined from deterministic relative geometry and collision risk. The resulting situation selects a situation-specific decision core, while the radar perception module is shared across the encounter experts. This structure reflects that the physical scene is common across COLREGs situations, whereas the appropriate control response differs by obligation. Communication is introduced only after an initial local-navigation stage, and a self-supervised threat-decoding objective encourages the compact message to retain collision-relevant information observed by the sender.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Communication-enabled cooperative navigation under partial observability:} A learned inter-vessel communication mechanism combines compact latent messages with receiver-side relative geometry and multi-neighbor aggregation. A varying number of neighboring messages are transformed into a fixed-dimensional cooperative representation without requiring a global state.

    \item \textbf{Shared-perception and situation-specific decision making:} The dominant COLREGs situation is obtained from deterministic encounter geometry and collision urgency. The radar perception module is shared across situations, whereas the downstream decision cores remain specialized for different navigational obligations.

    \item \textbf{Stable learning of a useful communication channel:} Communication is introduced after local navigation has been established, partner messages are regenerated during PPO updates so that receiver losses can train the sender MessageActor, and a ThreatDecoder encourages messages to preserve locally observed collision-relevant information.

    \item \textbf{Component-wise and behavior-level evaluation:} Controlled experiments isolate communication, expert sharing, neighbor aggregation, message width, COLREGs shaping, and communication timing. Additional scalability, trajectory, message-space, heterogeneous-fleet, and route-planning studies examine how the resulting cooperation behaves beyond the core ablations.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~2 introduces the vessel dynamics and COLREGs requirements. Section~3 reviews path planning and cooperative navigation for MASS. Section~4 presents the proposed cooperative navigation framework. Section~5 describes the simulation environment and evaluation protocol. Section~6 presents the experimental results and additional behavior-level analyses. Section~7 concludes the paper.
}

% =====================================================================
{\color{violet}
This section describes the vessel motion model and the COLREGs considered for autonomous navigation. The motion model characterizes the kinematic and dynamic behavior of a vessel, while the COLREGs specify the navigation rules for collision avoidance in different encounter situations.
}

\begin{figure}[!t]

\subsection{Vessel Dynamics}
{\color{violet}
Fig.~\ref{fig:vessel_model} shows the vessel control model. A vessel controls its motion through propulsion thrust and rudder angle, represented by the control vector $\boldsymbol{\tau}$. The vessel pose in the earth-fixed frame is defined as $\boldsymbol{\eta}=[x,y,\psi]^T$, where $x$ and $y$ denote the position and $\psi$ denotes the heading angle. The body-fixed velocity vector is defined as $\boldsymbol{v}=[u,v,r]^T$, where $u$, $v$, and $r$ denote surge velocity, sway velocity, and yaw rate, respectively.

The body-fixed velocity is transformed into the earth-fixed frame by the rotation matrix
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{T}(\psi)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\psi & -\sin\psi & 0\\
\sin\psi & \cos\psi & 0\\
0&0&1
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
and the vessel kinematics are expressed as
\begin{equation}
\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=\mathbf{T}(\psi)\boldsymbol{v}.
\label{eq:kinematics}
\end{equation}
The translational and rotational motion is conventionally written as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}+
\mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}+
\mathbf{D}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}+
\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=
\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\tau},
\label{eq:dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{M}$ is the inertia matrix, $\mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{v})$ represents Coriolis and centripetal effects, $\mathbf{D}(\boldsymbol{v})$ represents hydrodynamic damping, $\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\eta})$ represents restoring forces, and $\mathbf{B}$ maps the control inputs to the forces and moments acting on the vessel. The simulator restricts motion to the horizontal plane and represents the corresponding surge and yaw response through acceleration, deceleration, drag, and rudder-response parameters. The learned policy controls two normalized commands: thrust and rudder.
}

    \centering

\end{figure}

\subsection{Collision Avoidance and COLREGs}
{\color{violet}
The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea specify navigation rules for preventing collisions between vessels. Among these regulations, Rules~13--17 define the principal obligations associated with the encounter situations considered in this study. These situations are illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:colregs_background}. In an Overtaking encounter, the overtaking vessel keeps clear until it is finally past and clear. In a Head-On encounter, both vessels alter course to starboard. In a crossing encounter, the vessel that has the other vessel on its starboard side acts as the Give-Way vessel, whereas the other vessel is the Stand-On vessel. The Give-Way vessel should take early and substantial action to keep clear, while the Stand-On vessel should maintain course and speed unless collision avoidance requires intervention.

These rules specify pairwise obligations, but a vessel in a multi-vessel environment can hold different obligations relative to different neighbors. For example, the same vessel may act as a Give-Way vessel relative to one neighbor while acting as a Stand-On vessel relative to another.

Collision risk between two vessels can be characterized using the Distance at Closest Point of Approach (DCPA) and Time to Closest Point of Approach (TCPA). DCPA represents the predicted minimum separation between two vessels if their current courses and speeds are maintained, while TCPA represents the time remaining until the closest point of approach. A small DCPA indicates a close predicted encounter, while a small positive TCPA indicates that the closest approach will occur in the near future. These quantities jointly characterize the spatial and temporal urgency of an encounter and are commonly used for collision-risk assessment \citep{Zhao-COLREGs}.
}

\section{Related Work}
% =====================================================================
{\color{violet}
Related studies on MASS navigation can be broadly grouped into path planning and cooperative navigation. Path-planning studies focus on generating feasible routes and collision-avoidance trajectories for individual vessels, while cooperative-navigation studies consider how multiple vessels exchange information and coordinate their trajectories. This section reviews both research directions and identifies the limitations addressed by the proposed framework.

\subsection{Path Planning for MASS}
MASS path planning determines a sequence of positions and control actions that guide a vessel from its origin to a destination while satisfying navigational constraints. Global path planning identifies waypoints that define the overall route from the origin to the destination, whereas local path planning determines detailed trajectories between consecutive waypoints by accounting for real-time vessel dynamics, surrounding traffic, and collision risks \citep{Yuan}.

Global path-planning methods such as A* and Dijkstra's algorithm can determine optimal or near-optimal routes when an accurate map of the navigation environment is available \citep{58}. Data-driven approaches have also used AIS records to incorporate historical vessel movements and environmental conditions into route planning. Neural-network models have generated fuel-efficient routes from historical navigation data, and trajectory-prediction models have inferred intermediate waypoints from vessel movement histories \citep{119,120}. These methods improve route generation but do not directly resolve collision risks that emerge during navigation.

Local path planning addresses short-term changes by determining detailed trajectories according to nearby traffic and evolving encounter conditions. Artificial potential fields, velocity-obstacle methods, model predictive control, rule-based methods, and reinforcement learning have all been studied for real-time collision avoidance \citep{58,Sarhadi}. DRL is particularly useful when the mapping from multi-vessel observations to control actions is difficult to specify analytically. COLREGs-compliant DRL has demonstrated that encounter-dependent maritime rules can be incorporated into learned collision-avoidance policies \citep{Zhao-COLREGs}. Despite these advances, existing DRL approaches generally determine collision-avoidance actions independently for each vessel. Information acquired by different vessels is not directly exchanged to supplement their limited local observations.

\subsection{Cooperative MASS Navigation}
When vessels navigate independently, information about neighboring vessels is obtained primarily through onboard sensing and externally available traffic information. Although these sources provide observed motion states, they provide limited information about what other vessels observe locally and how an encounter may evolve outside the sensing range of an individual vessel. Cooperative navigation supplements this information through direct inter-vessel communication \citep{Akdag,chen}.

Communication strategies differ in the information exchanged among vessels. Existing studies have considered positional information and collision-risk measures, intended control actions, and historical trajectories for predicting future motion \citep{82,29}. More recent MARL approaches encode local observations into compact latent messages learned jointly with the navigation policy. These messages provide task-relevant representations of locally acquired information that neighboring agents can incorporate into their own observations \citep{WangZhao2025}.

Communication-enabled MARL has demonstrated improved cooperative vessel navigation in small multi-agent settings \citep{WangZhao2025}. Extending such methods to denser traffic creates two additional problems. First, a vessel may receive messages from several neighbors simultaneously, so their number and ordering cannot be allowed to change the policy-input structure. Second, multi-vessel COLREGs encounters can assign different obligations relative to different neighbors. The proposed framework therefore combines permutation-invariant multi-neighbor communication with deterministic COLREGs situation assessment and situation-specific decision functions. The communication channel is introduced after local navigation has been established to reduce the optimization difficulty caused by learning navigation and inter-agent communication simultaneously.
}

\section{Proposed Framework}
\label{sec:framework}
% =====================================================================
{\color{violet}
This section presents the proposed framework for cooperative MASS navigation. The multi-agent formulation is first introduced, followed by the observation representation, inter-vessel message passing, COLREGs situation assessment and expert structure, and the staged communication training used to learn cooperative navigation.
}

\subsection{System Overview}
\label{sec:system_overview}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.92\textwidth]{figure/fig6}
    \caption{Overall framework for communication-enabled cooperative MASS navigation.}
    \label{fig:framework_structure}
\end{figure*}

{\color{violet}
Consider $N$ vessel agents indexed by $\mathcal{I}=\{1,\ldots,N\}$. The cooperative navigation problem is modeled as a decentralized partially observable decision process in which each vessel acts from its own observation and messages received from neighboring vessels. The communication neighbors of agent $i$ are defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{N}_i=\{j\neq i:d_{ij}\le r_{\mathrm{comm}}\},
\label{eq:neighbor_set}
\end{equation}
where $d_{ij}$ is the distance between agents $i$ and $j$ and $r_{\mathrm{comm}}$ is the communication range.

Fig.~\ref{fig:framework_structure} illustrates the overall information flow. Each vessel employs a MessageActor and a ControlActor whose parameters are shared across the fleet. The MessageActor converts locally available information into a compact latent message. The ControlActor combines the receiving vessel's local features with messages from nearby vessels and generates the continuous rudder and thrust commands. A Critic is used during PPO optimization. Training is performed in parallel with shared parameters, whereas execution remains decentralized: each vessel requires only its local observation and messages received from communication neighbors.

The architecture separates three roles. Radar perception describes the same physical scene regardless of the active COLREGs obligation and is therefore shared across the situation experts. The control response differs among Head-On, Give-Way, Stand-On, Overtaking, and no-active-encounter situations and is therefore specialized. Information that is unavailable from the receiver's local observation is supplied through the learned communication channel. This separation provides the organizing principle for the following subsections.
}

\subsection{Observation Model}
\label{sec:observation}
{\color{violet}
At each decision step, the environment provides agent $i$ with radar measurements, goal information, its own dynamic state, absolute position, and the current dominant COLREGs situation. The raw environment observation is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{o}^{\mathrm{env}}_i=
[\mathbf{O}_{\mathrm{radar},i}\|\mathbf{O}_{\mathrm{goal},i}\|\mathbf{O}_{\mathrm{self},i}\|\mathbf{p}_i\|c_i]
\in\mathbb{R}^{369}.
\label{eq:env_obs}
\end{equation}
The radar observation $\mathbf{O}_{\mathrm{radar},i}\in\mathbb{R}^{360}$ represents a $360^\circ$ omnidirectional scan. The goal observation $\mathbf{O}_{\mathrm{goal},i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ contains the normalized distance and relative direction to the current goal. The self-state $\mathbf{O}_{\mathrm{self},i}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}$ describes the vessel's current motion and control state. The position $\mathbf{p}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is retained by the environment for communication geometry but is not used as a direct learned policy feature. The scalar situation index $c_i\in\{0,\ldots,4\}$ represents None, Head-On, Crossing Stand-On, Crossing Give-Way, or Overtaking.

Only the radar history is frame stacked. The latest three 360-ray scans are processed by a circular one-dimensional convolutional encoder,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}^{r}_i=E_r(\mathbf{R}_i),
\qquad \mathbf{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times360},
\label{eq:radar_encoder}
\end{equation}
which produces a 30-dimensional radar feature. Circular padding reflects the physical adjacency of the first and last radar rays. The goal distance is normalized as $d_g/(d_g+150)$ and the goal angle by $180^\circ$. The feature supplied to the MessageActor is then
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_i=
[\mathbf{z}^{r}_i\|\mathbf{O}_{\mathrm{goal},i}\|\mathbf{O}_{\mathrm{self},i}\|\operatorname{onehot}(c_i)]
\in\mathbb{R}^{41}.
\label{eq:local_feature}
\end{equation}
Thus, the 369-dimensional environment observation and the 41-dimensional learned local feature describe different stages of the processing pipeline. The former is the simulator interface, whereas the latter is the compact representation used by the neural decision modules.
}

\subsection{Message Passing}
\label{sec:communication}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.90\textwidth]{figure/fig7}
    \caption{Message-passing architecture for cooperative navigation. A sender generates a compact latent message, and the receiver associates the message with relative geometry before combining information from multiple neighbors.}
    \label{fig:message_architecture}
\end{figure*}
% TODO(FIGURE-LABEL): retain the existing diagram; replace obsolete sum-aggregation and input-dimension labels with the current grounded masked-mean pipeline.

{\color{violet}
Fig.~\ref{fig:message_architecture} illustrates the message-passing architecture. Each agent first encodes its local feature into a compact latent message. The MessageActor $M_{\theta_M}$ generates
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m}_i=M_{\theta_M}(\mathbf{x}_i)\in[-1,1]^6.
\label{eq:message}
\end{equation}
The six-dimensional message represents information learned from the sender's local observation. Its physical origin is not left for the latent channel to infer. Instead, the receiver explicitly computes the relative geometry of each sender.

For sender $j$ and receiver $i$, the relative feature is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}_{ij}=\left[\sin\phi_{ij},\cos\phi_{ij},d_{ij}/r_{\mathrm{comm}}\right],
\label{eq:relative_geometry}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_{ij}$ and $d_{ij}$ are the relative bearing and distance. The receiver combines this geometry with the incoming message as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_{ij}=f_{\mathrm{msg}}([\mathbf{m}_j\|\mathbf{r}_{ij}]),
\label{eq:grounded_message}
\end{equation}
where $f_{\mathrm{msg}}$ is a shared message encoder. Among vessels within the 420~m communication range, the nearest four are retained. Their grounded messages are aggregated by a masked mean,
\begin{equation}
\bar{\mathbf{m}}_i=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_i|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_i}\mathbf{e}_{ij},
\label{eq:message_aggregation}
\end{equation}
with $\bar{\mathbf{m}}_i=\mathbf{0}$ when no valid partner exists. This operation converts a varying number of neighboring messages into a fixed six-dimensional representation and is invariant to neighbor ordering. Because relative geometry is incorporated before averaging, the information received from different directions is not represented as an unordered latent sum without physical context.

The aggregated message is combined with the receiving vessel's local features and supplied to the ControlActor and Critic. A learnable scalar gate scales the communication feature before it enters the downstream decision network. The ControlActor produces a bounded two-dimensional continuous action corresponding to normalized rudder and thrust commands. A squashed Gaussian policy is used during PPO training.
}

\subsection{COLREGs Situation Assessment and Shared-Perception MoE}
\label{sec:colregs_routing}
\label{sec:shared_moe}
{\color{violet}
In a multi-vessel environment, agent $i$ may simultaneously encounter several vessels and hold different COLREGs obligations relative to each of them. The framework therefore separates \emph{situation assessment} from \emph{policy specialization}. Situation assessment is deterministic and is based on relative geometry and collision urgency; the resulting situation index then selects the corresponding decision core of the MoE.

For each neighboring vessel detected within the 56~m COLREGs assessment range, the pairwise encounter is first screened using relative bearing and current motion. Pairs that are clearly behind the own vessel, have already passed, or have negative TCPA are excluded from active consideration. The remaining geometry is classified as Head-On, Crossing Stand-On, Crossing Give-Way, or Overtaking. The implementation also uses a near-axis rule to avoid unstable switching between crossing classes when the relative bearing is close to zero.

The urgency of each active pair combines current distance, TCPA, and DCPA. In compact form,
\begin{equation}
\rho_{ij}=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}
\left[\beta_{c_{ij}}(0.3r_d+0.4r_{\tau}+0.3r_{\delta})\right],
\label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}
where $r_d$, $r_{\tau}$, and $r_{\delta}$ are normalized distance-, TCPA-, and DCPA-based risk terms. The encounter multiplier $\beta_{c_{ij}}$ is 2.0 for Head-On, 1.5 for Crossing Give-Way, 1.3 for Crossing Stand-On, and 1.2 for Overtaking. When several active pairs are present, the situation associated with the highest-risk pair becomes the dominant COLREGs context $c_i$.

The neural architecture uses this known maritime context as a hard routing variable rather than learning a separate soft gate. For each of the MessageActor, ControlActor, and Critic, the radar perception encoder is shared across the five contexts, whereas the downstream decision core is situation specific. The resulting operation can be written as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_i=F_{c_i}([E_r(\mathbf{R}_i)\|\mathbf{u}_i]),
\label{eq:shared_moe}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{u}_i$ denotes the remaining goal, self-state, and, for the ControlActor and Critic, communication features. This structure avoids learning five separate copies of the same physical perception problem while allowing opposite COLREGs obligations to retain different control mappings.

The distinction is important because the radar encoder dominates the parameter count of the single-network implementation. Fully separating the complete perception pipeline across all situations therefore increases model size substantially and splits perception training data by encounter type. Sharing the perception module preserves a common representation of the surrounding scene, while the situation-specific cores specialize only the part of the policy that needs to differ.
}

\subsection{Staged Communication Training}
\label{sec:staged_training}
{\color{violet}
Policy training becomes more difficult when cooperative communication is introduced before basic navigation behavior has been sufficiently established. At the beginning of training, the MessageActor produces an unstable representation while the ControlActor must simultaneously learn how to reach the goal, avoid collisions, and interpret that evolving representation. A staged schedule is therefore used to separate the acquisition of basic navigation from the later coupling between vessels.

Training proceeds as one continuous 16-million-decision run. During the first 9 million decisions, incoming communication is set to zero. Each vessel therefore learns goal-directed navigation, collision avoidance, and the COLREGs-aware control behavior using only locally available features. The learned policy parameters are not reset at the stage boundary.

After 9 million decisions, inter-vessel communication is activated and training continues with the same policy parameters. The MessageActor begins generating messages that are used by neighboring ControlActors, and the agents learn how locally unavailable information can be incorporated into cooperative navigation decisions. The COLREGs shaping coefficient remains unchanged across the two stages. Thus, the schedule does not introduce a separate third COLREGs phase; its purpose is specifically to delay the coupling created by learned communication until a usable local navigation policy has already formed.

A separate communication-from-start condition is evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:timing_result}. That experiment distinguishes the benefit of communication itself from the optimization effect of delaying when the communication channel becomes active.
}

\subsection{Communication Credit Assignment and Threat Decoding}
\label{sec:comm_learning}
{\color{red}
Activating a forward message path is not sufficient for learning useful communication. During PPO optimization, the receiver's policy loss must remain connected to the MessageActor that generated the sender's message. The rollout buffer therefore stores the partner observations associated with each received message. During the PPO update, partner messages are regenerated using the current MessageActor and aggregated again using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:relative_geometry}--\eqref{eq:message_aggregation}. The receiver-side policy loss can consequently update the parameters that produced its neighbors' messages instead of treating the messages as fixed rollout data.

A self-supervised ThreatDecoder provides an additional representation-learning signal. For each sender, the three nearest radar threats are described by bearing, normalized distance, and closing information. The decoder predicts these threat descriptors from the six-dimensional message,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{th}}_i=D_{\mathrm{th}}(\mathbf{m}_i),
\end{equation}
and a masked $L_1$ loss is applied only to valid threat slots. The reported experiments use a threat-decoder coefficient of 0.5. This auxiliary objective encourages the transmitted message to retain collision-relevant information available to the sender, but it does not prescribe the action that the receiving vessel should take.
}

\subsection{Reward and PPO Optimization}
\label{sec:training}
{\color{red}
The reward used to generate the principal Fig.~\ref{fig:comm_necessity}--Fig.~\ref{fig:comm_timing} checkpoints is organized into four functional components,
\begin{equation}
R_i=R_i^{\mathrm{task}}+R_i^{\mathrm{safety}}+R_i^{\mathrm{eff}}+R_i^{\mathrm{COLREGs}}.
\label{eq:reward_groups}
\end{equation}
The task component contains arrival, time, and goal-progress terms. The safety component contains collision, proximity, collision-course, and pairwise-risk penalties. The efficiency component penalizes control effort and abrupt rudder motion while encouraging forward and goal-directed travel. The COLREGs component is a directional noncompliance penalty. Head-On, Give-Way, and Overtaking situations penalize port-side steering, whereas Stand-On penalizes unnecessary course change. The reported common configuration uses a COLREGs coefficient of 0.45.

This representation is intentionally kept simple in the manuscript. The reward is not treated as a new analytical model; it supplies task, safety, efficiency, and rule-consistency signals to the learned policy. The implementation-level coefficients used to reproduce the archived experiments remain in the project configuration and figure-generation records.

The policy is optimized using PPO \citep{PPO}. The implementation uses discount factor $\gamma=0.99$, generalized-advantage parameter 0.95, learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, PPO clip parameter 0.2, two optimization epochs per rollout, minibatch size 512, entropy coefficient 0.01, value coefficient 0.5, and gradient clipping at 0.5.
}

\section{Simulation Environment and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:simulation}
% =====================================================================
{\color{red}
The principal numerical experiments are generated with the GPU-batched simulator \texttt{vessel\_gym.py}. The simulator reproduces the vessel dynamics, radar sensing, collision geometry, COLREGs situation logic, and observation contract used by the Unity/C\# reference implementation while allowing many environments to run in parallel. The Unity implementation is retained for visualization and reference behavior, but the quantitative results reported here are generated by the batched simulator.

The navigation arena is $600\times600$~m and contains nine circular obstacles of radius 20~m arranged on a $3\times3$ grid. Each environment contains 16 vessels. A ring factor of 0.7 scales the spawn and goal geometry, producing an average direct trip of approximately 373~m. Initial headings point approximately toward the assigned goals, and each vessel receives an independently sampled maximum-speed multiplier.

Radar range is 56~m and communication range is 420~m. At most four communication partners are retained. Physics is integrated at 0.04~s with ten substeps per policy decision, giving one control decision every 0.4~s. Rudder command is bounded by $30^\circ$, the physical rudder slew rate is $12^\circ$/s, and goal arrival is declared within 3~m. The common training configuration uses 128 parallel environments, rollout length 32, and seeds 42, 43, and 44.

After training, checkpoints are frozen and evaluated by repeated navigation rollouts. Primary metrics are arrival rate, collision rate, COLREGs compliance, fuel/control-effort accumulation, heading travel, minimum vessel separation, and episode length. Learning-log scores and frozen-policy evaluation metrics are treated as different protocols and are not mixed in the same quantitative comparison.

For the message-dimension study, the archived analysis also reports the outcome score
\begin{equation}

\label{eq:outcome_score}
\end{equation}
which is used only as a compact evaluation summary and is not the training reward.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
% =====================================================================
{\color{red}
The evaluation is organized around design questions. The first six figures isolate the main architectural and training choices. The subsequent scalability, trajectory, and message-space analyses examine how the learned cooperation behaves, while the final studies consider heterogeneous communication capability and integration with route-level planning.
}

\subsection{Effect of Inter-Vessel Communication}
\label{sec:comm_result}
\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.96\textwidth]{paper_figures/Fig1_Communication_necessity}
    \caption{Effect of inter-vessel communication under the proposed shared-perception architecture.}
    \label{fig:comm_necessity}
\end{figure*}
{\color{red}
Fig.~\ref{fig:comm_necessity} compares the same policy architecture with communication disabled and enabled. Collision rate decreases from 2.77\% to 1.83\%, while aggregate COLREGs compliance increases from 48.4\% to 52.3\%. Arrival changes only modestly, from 54.4\% to 55.6\%, showing that the safety gain is not obtained by simply reducing task completion. Fuel/control-effort accumulation and heading travel also decrease, indicating smoother rather than more aggressive collision avoidance.

The situation-specific results show that the benefit is not uniform across encounter classes. Head-On compliance is already close to saturation without communication, while Give-Way and Stand-On improve more strongly. Overtaking decreases from 76\% to 70\%. The appropriate conclusion is therefore that communication is particularly useful for interaction-dependent crossing encounters, not that it improves every metric in every situation.
}

\subsection{Shared-Perception MoE Architecture}
\label{sec:moe_result}
\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.97\textwidth]{paper_figures/Fig2_MoE_architecture}
    \caption{Comparison of a single network, parameter-matched thin separated MoE, full-width separated MoE, and the proposed shared-perception MoE.}
    \label{fig:moe_architecture}
\end{figure*}
{\color{red}
Fig.~\ref{fig:moe_architecture} tests where situation specialization should occur. The single network contains 369,131 parameters. The thin separated MoE contains 363,004 parameters, the full-width separated MoE grows to 1,826,719 parameters, and the proposed shared-perception model requires 511,543 parameters.

The single network reaches 57.2\% arrival with 6.07\% collision, whereas the proposed model reaches 55.6\% arrival with 1.83\% collision. The thin separated model performs substantially worse, indicating that dividing a fixed parameter budget across complete expert networks fragments representational capacity. The full separated model restores capacity but remains much larger and does not match the proposed safety result. These results support sharing physical perception while specializing the downstream decision function. The thin condition currently has one completed seed and is therefore interpreted as a structural comparison rather than a precise multi-seed effect-size estimate.
}

\subsection{Multi-Neighbor Message Aggregation}
\label{sec:aggregation_result}
\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.96\textwidth]{paper_figures/Fig3_Multi_neighbour_aggregation}
    \caption{Nearest-one versus four-neighbor position-grounded message aggregation.}
    \label{fig:multi_neighbor}
\end{figure*}
{\color{red}
Fig.~\ref{fig:multi_neighbor} changes the number of retained communication partners. Using four neighbors reduces collision from 3.43\% to 1.83\% and raises aggregate COLREGs compliance from 45.6\% to 52.3\%. Fuel/control effort and heading travel also decrease.

This behavior is consistent with the limitation of solving one pairwise conflict at a time. An action that resolves the nearest encounter can move the own ship toward another vessel whose information has been discarded. Aggregating several spatially grounded messages allows the controller to respond to a local traffic configuration rather than repeatedly switching between isolated pairs.
}

\subsection{Message Dimensionality}
\label{sec:dimension_result}
\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.94\textwidth]{paper_figures/Fig4_Message_dimensionality}
    \caption{Sensitivity to latent message dimension. This sweep uses the full-width separated MoE and is interpreted as a channel-capacity study.}
    \label{fig:message_dimension}
\end{figure*}
{\color{red}
Fig.~\ref{fig:message_dimension} sweeps the message dimension over $\{2,4,6,8,10,12\}$. Very small channels degrade performance, while the sequence beyond the moderate-dimensional regime is not monotonic. Dimension 8 gives the highest measured outcome score and dimension 12 gives the highest measured aggregate compliance, so the data do not support a unique optimum.

The useful conclusion is that the channel must be large enough to avoid a severe information bottleneck, whereas increasing width beyond a moderate capacity does not provide a systematic gain. Dimension 6 is retained in the principal matched experiments as a compact operating point. Because this sweep was generated with the full-width separated MoE, it is treated as a communication-capacity study rather than a direct ablation of the final shared-perception architecture.
}

\subsection{Effect of COLREGs Shaping}
\label{sec:colregs_result}
\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.94\textwidth]{paper_figures/Fig5_COLREGs_shaping}
    \caption{Effect of directional COLREGs shaping on collision avoidance and rule compliance.}
    \label{fig:colregs_shaping}
\end{figure*}
{\color{red}
Fig.~\ref{fig:colregs_shaping} compares the proposed shared-perception policy with the directional COLREGs coefficient set to 0 and 0.45. In the matched three-seed comparison, aggregate compliance increases from approximately 31.4\% to 52.3\% and collision decreases from approximately 6.43\% to 1.83\%.

The collision reduction is important because the COLREGs term does more than change the compliance score. It supplies a common maneuver convention: Head-On, Give-Way, and Overtaking discourage port turns, whereas Stand-On discourages unnecessary course change. This breaks a symmetric failure mode in which individually cautious agents choose mutually incompatible avoidance directions. The shaping term therefore acts as a domain-informed coordination prior.
}

\subsection{Communication-Introduction Timing}
\label{sec:timing_result}
\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.92\textwidth]{paper_figures/Fig6_Communication_timing}
    \caption{Comparison of communication from the start of training and communication introduced after 9 million decisions.}
    \label{fig:comm_timing}
\end{figure*}
{\color{red}
Fig.~\ref{fig:comm_timing} separates the value of communication from the schedule used to learn it. Communication from the first decision produces 61.3\% mean arrival but 6.53\% collision and 46.4\% compliance. Delayed communication produces 55.6\% arrival, 1.83\% collision, and 52.3\% compliance. The arrival rate of communication-from-start is also highly seed sensitive, with approximately $\pm14.5$ percentage points of variation compared with $\pm1.4$ for delayed activation.

The result is therefore a safety-and-stability result, not a claim that delayed communication improves every metric. Establishing a usable local navigation policy before coupling the agents through a learned communication channel produces a safer and substantially less seed-sensitive solution.
}

\label{sec:scalability_result}

{\color{red}
The preceding experiments use 16 vessels and establish the benefit of the proposed design at one traffic density. Fig.~\ref{fig:scalability} addresses a different question: whether the learned cooperative structure remains useful as the number of simultaneously navigating vessels changes.

Two notions of scalability are relevant. Representation scalability follows directly from the architecture. Policy parameters are shared among vessels, at most four neighbors are retained, and the aggregated message remains six dimensional. The input dimension therefore does not increase with fleet size. Behavioral scalability must instead be measured because a denser fleet creates more simultaneous collision conflicts even when the network size remains fixed.

The final rerun of this experiment will compare the communication-ON and communication-OFF policies at 4, 8, and 16 vessels under the current simulator configuration. The main quantity of interest is the relative degradation of collision, compliance, and arrival as traffic density increases. A slower degradation with communication would show that bounded local information exchange remains useful as interaction density grows; it would not constitute an asymptotic computational-complexity claim. The existing figure is retained here so that the manuscript structure and interpretation remain fixed while the matched numerical curves are regenerated.

The proposed learned policy is a local cooperative controller rather than a global voyage planner. \subsection{Overall Interpretation}
{\color{red}
The component-wise experiments support a consistent interpretation of the proposed design. Communication addresses information missing from local sensing. Relative geometry preserves the physical origin of a learned message before multi-neighbor aggregation. Shared perception avoids replicating the dominant representation cost across COLREGs situations, while situation-specific decision cores retain behavior that should differ among maritime obligations. Directional COLREGs shaping supplies a common maneuver convention, and delayed communication reduces the optimization instability created by coupling agents before local navigation has stabilized.

The scalability, trajectory, and message-space analyses answer different questions from the ablations. Fleet-size evaluation asks whether the cooperation remains useful as the number of simultaneous interactions changes while the neural input dimension stays fixed. Trajectory analysis connects the aggregate safety benefit to the timing and smoothness of the realized maneuver. Message-space analysis examines whether the six-dimensional bottleneck retains structured threat and navigation information rather than merely reproducing a situation label. The heterogeneous-fleet and A* studies then extend the evaluation toward mixed communication capability and integration with conventional global planning.
}

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================
{\color{red}
This paper presented a communication-enabled framework for cooperative MASS navigation under partial observability. Each vessel converts its local observation into a compact latent message, receiver-side relative geometry preserves the physical origin of the transmitted information, and a fixed-dimensional aggregation allows several neighboring vessels to contribute without changing the policy-input size. Deterministic COLREGs situation assessment selects situation-specific decision functions while a shared radar encoder avoids unnecessary duplication of physical perception.

The controlled experiments show that the individual design choices address different failure modes. Communication lowers collision rate and particularly improves crossing coordination. Aggregating several neighbors is safer than retaining only the nearest vessel. Sharing perception provides a better safety--parameter tradeoff than fully duplicating the expert networks. Directional COLREGs shaping functions as a coordination prior, and introducing communication after local navigation has been established yields lower collision and substantially lower seed sensitivity than activating the communication channel from the beginning of training.

Fleet-size, trajectory, and message-space analyses are retained as part of the main evaluation because they connect the component ablations to density robustness, realized avoidance behavior, and the learned communication representation. Their existing artwork will be regenerated from the current checkpoint family before submission so that all numerical annotations follow the same simulator configuration. Future work will incorporate explicit communication latency, packet loss, and bandwidth constraints, train directly with route-level waypoint sequences, and validate the learned controller in higher-fidelity maritime traffic environments.
}
