\begin{abstract}
{\color{violet}
Cooperative navigation among Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS) is challenging in partially observable maritime environments, where limited local sensing restricts situational awareness during multi-vessel encounters. This paper develops a communication-enabled cooperative navigation framework for coordinated path planning and collision avoidance. Each vessel encodes its local observation into a compact latent message, while messages from multiple neighboring vessels are combined with relative-position information and aggregated into a fixed-dimensional cooperative representation. A COLREGs-conditioned Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture hard-routes each navigation state to a situation-specific decision core while sharing the high-dimensional radar perception module across experts. This structure preserves situation-specific control behavior without replicating the dominant perception cost for every expert. Communication is introduced only after a local navigation policy has first been established, and a self-supervised threat-decoding objective encourages the latent message to retain collision-relevant information observed by the transmitting vessel.

The framework is evaluated in a GPU-batched maritime simulator that reproduces the vessel dynamics, radar sensing, collision geometry, and COLREGs logic of the Unity reference implementation. Controlled experiments with 16 simultaneously navigating vessels isolate the effects of communication, multi-neighbor aggregation, message dimensionality, COLREGs shaping, communication-introduction timing, and expert sharing. Communication reduces collision rate and control effort while improving COLREGs compliance, and the shared-perception MoE achieves substantially lower collision rate than both a single network and fully separated experts at a fraction of the parameter count of the latter. These results demonstrate that communication-enabled distributed intelligence can improve cooperative navigation under partial observability while retaining a decentralized execution structure.
}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
{\color{violet}
The rapid advances of autonomous navigation technologies have brought Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS) to the forefront of maritime research and regulation. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) has examined the operation of MASS through its regulatory scoping exercise, reflecting growing interest in remotely controlled and fully autonomous vessels \cite{1}. Despite this progress, safe and efficient navigation in congested waters remains a major challenge to the practical deployment of MASS \cite{2}. Autonomous vessels need to avoid collisions while complying with the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs), particularly in dense maritime traffic where multiple vessels may interact simultaneously under uncertain and changing conditions \cite{3}.

Cooperative navigation involves multiple vessels coordinating their trajectories and sharing locally acquired information to improve navigational safety and efficiency \cite{4}. Each vessel observes only part of the surrounding environment and exchanges locally available information with neighboring vessels. The received information is combined with the vessel's own observation to determine its subsequent navigation behavior. Instead of relying on a complete global representation of the maritime environment, cooperative navigation distributes locally acquired information among vessels so that their trajectories can be coordinated according to surrounding traffic conditions.

Communication-enabled cooperative navigation involves three key operations. First, each vessel extracts navigation-relevant information from its local observation and encodes it into a compact message. Second, messages received from neighboring vessels within communication range are aggregated into a fixed-dimensional cooperative representation. Third, each vessel determines its trajectory by combining this representation with its own local observation. This information exchange extends situational awareness beyond individual sensing ranges and supports coordinated responses to potential conflicts \cite{5,6}. This communication distinguishes cooperative navigation from independent collision avoidance, where surrounding vessels are treated as moving obstacles and each vessel determines its trajectory solely from locally available observations.

Classical path-planning techniques include graph-search algorithms such as A* and Dijkstra's algorithm, sampling-based planners, and artificial potential field methods \cite{8}. Graph-search methods can determine optimal or near-optimal routes with respect to a predefined discretization and cost function, whereas potential field methods provide computationally efficient local guidance. However, these methods rely on predefined representations of the navigation environment and do not directly resolve the coupled interactions among multiple autonomous vessels \cite{10}. Model Predictive Control (MPC) incorporates vessel dynamics, operational constraints, and short-term motion predictions into online trajectory planning \cite{11}. Its computational cost generally increases with the number of interacting vessels and the prediction horizon, which complicates its application to dense multi-vessel environments \cite{12}. Rule-based methods explicitly encode COLREGs requirements, but their navigation behavior is largely determined by predefined encounter categories and may become difficult to specify for multi-vessel encounter configurations \cite{13}.

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) provides a learning-based approach to autonomous vessel navigation beyond predefined planning and encounter rules. Through repeated interactions with simulated maritime environments, an autonomous vessel can learn a collision-avoidance policy without explicitly programming every possible encounter and avoidance maneuver \cite{16,17,18}. Existing studies have shown that DRL can jointly account for goal reaching, collision avoidance, and COLREGs compliance within a learned navigation policy \cite{19,20}. However, most single-agent DRL approaches model surrounding vessels as dynamic obstacles rather than cooperative agents. These vessels neither exchange information nor coordinate their trajectories, leaving each agent to navigate solely from observations available within its own sensing range. This formulation limits situational awareness and does not support cooperative collision avoidance.

Cooperative navigation introduces additional learning challenges in multi-agent environments. As the policy of each vessel evolves during training, the transition dynamics observed by one agent change with the policies of other agents, creating a non-stationary learning environment that can destabilize joint policy learning \cite{21}. Partial observability further limits the information available to each vessel \cite{22}. Radar, cameras, and other onboard sensors have finite ranges and fields of view, and their measurements can be affected by occlusion, environmental conditions, and sensor uncertainty \cite{23,24}. As a result, each vessel determines its navigation behavior using incomplete information about the surrounding maritime traffic.

Inter-vessel communication can alleviate partial observability by exchanging information acquired locally by individual vessels \cite{25}. Direct transmission of raw observations, however, increases communication overhead and may convey information irrelevant to the current navigation task. Learned communication instead encodes the local observation of each vessel into a compact, task-relevant message optimized jointly with the navigation policy \cite{26,27}. Communication alone, however, does not ensure effective cooperation. Coordinated navigation depends on the information encoded in each message, the aggregation of messages from a varying number of neighbors, and the use of the aggregated information in selecting navigation actions. These factors become increasingly important in dense maritime traffic, where several vessels may simultaneously enter communication range and create interacting collision risks.

Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) extends reinforcement learning to environments where multiple agents learn and act concurrently \cite{31}. In cooperative MARL, agents learn policies that coordinate their actions toward a shared objective, with applications including cooperative collision avoidance, formation control \cite{32}, task allocation \cite{33}, and multi-vessel path planning \cite{34}. Communication-enabled MARL further incorporates the exchange of observations or learned messages to supplement information unavailable from local observations \cite{35}. For example, Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (MADDPG) with explicit inter-agent communication has been applied to cooperative navigation and collision avoidance \cite{36}. Existing studies, however, have largely focused on small-scale multi-vessel settings. When a vessel receives messages from several neighbors simultaneously, the messages vary in number and order. Direct concatenation produces an input dimension that depends on the number of neighbors, whereas unstructured aggregation can discard the physical origin of a message. A multi-vessel method therefore needs a permutation-invariant representation that also preserves which relative direction and distance each message refers to.

COLREGs compliance presents another challenge in multi-vessel environments. A vessel may simultaneously act as the give-way vessel relative to one neighbor and the stand-on vessel relative to another \cite{38,39}. The policy must therefore represent behaviorally different obligations without allowing one frequent encounter type to dominate the representation learned for another. In addition, learned communication is difficult to optimize if the message production path and the receiver's control path are not connected by a valid policy-gradient computation.

To address these challenges, this paper develops a communication-enabled cooperative navigation framework with three coupled design elements. First, each vessel generates a six-dimensional latent message, and up to four neighbor messages are grounded by their relative bearing and distance before masked-mean aggregation. Second, the navigation networks use a COLREGs-conditioned hard-routed MoE in which the radar perception module is shared across the five situation experts and only the decision cores remain situation-specific. Third, communication learning is stabilized by preserving sender-to-receiver gradients during PPO updates and by a self-supervised threat-decoding objective that forces messages to retain collision-relevant information observed by the sender. Communication is activated after an initial local-navigation stage rather than from the first training step.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Position-grounded multi-neighbor communication:} A learned six-dimensional message is combined with the sender's relative bearing and distance at the receiver and transformed before permutation-invariant aggregation. This keeps the transmitted representation compact while preserving the spatial attribution needed for multi-vessel collision avoidance.

    \item \textbf{Shared-perception, situation-specific MoE:} Five COLREGs-conditioned decision experts are hard-routed by deterministic encounter geometry, while the parameter-dominant RadarEncoder is shared across experts. This separates perception from situation-specific decision making and avoids the parameter explosion and data fragmentation of fully separated experts.

    \item \textbf{Stable communication learning with explicit credit assignment:} PPO updates recompute partner messages from stored partner observations so that the receiver's control objective propagates back to the transmitting MessageActor. A self-supervised ThreatDecoder additionally encourages each message to encode the sender's locally observed collision threats without directly constraining the receiving policy.

    \item \textbf{Controlled component-wise evaluation:} Communication, expert sharing, neighbor aggregation, message dimension, COLREGs shaping, and communication-introduction timing are evaluated by changing one experimental axis at a time under otherwise matched settings. The evaluation separates fixed-policy navigation metrics from learning-curve diagnostics and reports multi-seed variability.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the vessel dynamics and COLREGs requirements. Section 3 reviews path-planning and cooperative-navigation methods for MASS. Section 4 presents the proposed cooperative navigation framework and its learning procedure. Section 5 describes the simulation and evaluation protocols. Section 6 presents the experimental results and ablation studies, and Section 7 concludes the paper.
}

\subsection{Vessel Dynamics}
{\color{violet}
Fig.~\ref{fig:example1} shows a vessel control model. A vessel controls its motion through propulsion thrust and rudder angle, represented by the control vector $\boldsymbol{\tau}$. The vessel pose in the earth-fixed frame is defined as $\boldsymbol{\eta}=[x,y,\psi]^T$, where $x$ and $y$ denote the position and $\psi$ denotes the heading angle. The body-fixed velocity vector is defined as $\boldsymbol{v}=[u,v,r]^T$, where $u$, $v$, and $r$ denote surge velocity, sway velocity, and yaw rate, respectively.
The body-fixed velocity is transformed into the earth-fixed frame by
\begin{align}
\mathbf{T}(\psi)
=\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\psi & -\sin\psi & 0 \\
\sin\psi & \cos\psi & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:T}
\end{align}
and the vessel kinematics are
\begin{align}
\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=\mathbf{T}(\psi)\boldsymbol{v}.
\label{eq:kinematics}
\end{align}
The translational and rotational motion is generally written as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}
+\mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}
+\mathbf{D}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}
+\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
=\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\tau},
\label{eq:dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{M}$ is the inertia matrix, $\mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{v})$ is the Coriolis and centripetal matrix, $\mathbf{D}(\boldsymbol{v})$ represents hydrodynamic damping, $\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\eta})$ represents hydrostatic restoring forces, and $\mathbf{B}$ maps control inputs to forces and moments. In the simulator used in this work, motion is restricted to the horizontal plane and represented by surge and yaw dynamics using the acceleration, deceleration, drag, and rudder-response parameters summarized in Section~\ref{sec:simulation}.
}

\subsection{Collision Avoidance and COLREGs}
{\color{violet}
The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs) specify navigation rules for preventing collisions between vessels. Among these regulations, Rules~13--17 define the principal obligations associated with the encounter situations considered in this study. These situations are illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:example2} and summarized below.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Overtaking (Rule~13):} An overtaking vessel keeps clear of the vessel being overtaken until it is finally past and clear.
    \item \textbf{Head-On (Rule~14):} When two vessels approach on reciprocal or nearly reciprocal courses, each vessel alters its course to starboard so that they pass on the port side of each other.
    \item \textbf{Crossing (Rule~15):} In a crossing encounter, the vessel that has the other vessel on its starboard side acts as the give-way vessel and keeps clear.
    \item \textbf{Give-Way Vessel (Rule~16):} The give-way vessel takes early and substantial action to keep clear of the other vessel.
    \item \textbf{Stand-On Vessel (Rule~17):} The stand-on vessel maintains its course and speed but may take action to avoid collision when the give-way vessel does not take appropriate action.
\end{itemize}

In a multi-vessel environment, one vessel may simultaneously have different pairwise obligations relative to several neighbors. Collision risk is characterized using Distance at Closest Point of Approach (DCPA) and Time to Closest Point of Approach (TCPA). DCPA represents the predicted minimum separation if the current courses and speeds are maintained, whereas TCPA represents the time remaining until that closest approach. These quantities are used by the deterministic encounter assessment described in Section~\ref{sec:colregs_assessment}.
}

\section{Related Work}
{\color{violet}
Related studies on MASS navigation can be broadly grouped into path planning and cooperative navigation. Path-planning studies focus on generating feasible routes and collision-avoidance trajectories for individual vessels, while cooperative-navigation studies consider how multiple vessels exchange information and coordinate their trajectories. This section reviews both research directions and identifies the limitations addressed by the proposed framework.
}

\subsection{Path Planning for MASS}
{\color{violet}
MASS path planning determines a sequence of positions and control actions that guide a vessel from its origin to a destination while satisfying navigational constraints. Global path planning identifies waypoints that define the overall route from the origin to the destination, whereas local path planning determines detailed trajectories between consecutive waypoints by accounting for real-time vessel dynamics, surrounding traffic, and collision risks \citep{Yuan}.

Global path-planning methods such as A*, Dijkstra's algorithm, and geodesic-based routing can determine optimal or near-optimal routes when an accurate map of the navigation environment is available \citep{58}. A finer map representation improves route planning but increases the search space and computational cost. Data-driven approaches have used Automatic Identification System (AIS) records to incorporate historical vessel movements and environmental conditions into route planning. Neural-network models have generated fuel-efficient routes in Arctic waters using historical speed, course, and ice-concentration data \citep{119}. LSTM-based models have also predicted intermediate waypoints from vessel turning-point data \citep{120}. These methods use historical navigation data to improve route generation, but they do not directly respond to collision risks that emerge during navigation.

Local path planning addresses short-term changes by determining detailed trajectories between consecutive waypoints according to nearby traffic and evolving encounter conditions. Artificial potential fields, the dynamic window approach, and model predictive control have been widely studied for real-time collision avoidance \citep{58,Xue2024}. Their computational burden can increase as the number of interacting vessels grows. DRL provides a learning-based approach in which collision-avoidance behavior is acquired by maximizing cumulative rewards through repeated interactions with simulated maritime environments. Existing studies incorporate goal reaching, collision avoidance, and COLREGs compliance into the reward structure. Encounter classifiers have been used to identify the applicable COLREGs situation and activate corresponding rule-specific terms \citep{Zhao-COLREGs}. Despite these advances, existing DRL approaches generally determine collision-avoidance actions independently for each vessel and do not exchange task-dependent latent information among neighboring vessels.
}

\subsection{Cooperative MASS Navigation}
{\color{violet}
When vessels navigate independently, information about neighboring vessels is obtained primarily through onboard sensing and externally available traffic information such as AIS data. Although these sources provide observed motion states, they provide limited information about future navigation intentions. Cooperative navigation supplements this information through inter-vessel communication. Existing studies have considered positional information and collision-risk measures such as CPA \citep{58}, control intentions including rudder commands and speed changes \citep{82}, historical trajectories \citep{29}, and learned latent messages \citep{WangZhao2025}.

\citet{WangZhao2025} applied explicit message passing to cooperative vessel navigation using MADDPG and reported improved collision-avoidance performance over single-agent baselines. However, the study considers a two-vessel environment, where each vessel receives information from only one neighbor. In a multi-vessel environment, a receiving vessel must combine a varying number of messages without making the policy input depend on their order. It must also preserve enough spatial context to distinguish, for example, an approaching starboard-side vessel from a port-side vessel that generated a similar latent message.

The proposed framework addresses these limitations through relative-position-grounded multi-neighbor aggregation and a hard-routed shared-perception MoE. Unlike a learned MoE gate, the routing variable is the deterministic COLREGs situation obtained from encounter geometry. The high-dimensional radar encoder is shared across situation experts, while the downstream decision cores remain separate. Communication is trained jointly with the receiver through explicit sender-to-receiver gradient propagation rather than treating generated messages as detached features.
}

\section{Proposed Framework}
\label{sec:framework}
{\color{red}
This section describes the implementation used by the reported experiments. The description is organized around four operations performed at each navigation decision: perception, COLREGs-conditioned routing, inter-vessel message exchange, and continuous control. The final part describes how these operations are trained by PPO without breaking the gradient path from a receiving vessel's control objective to the messages generated by its neighbors.
}

\subsection{System Overview}
\label{sec:system_overview}
{\color{red}
Consider $N$ vessel agents indexed by $\mathcal{I}=\{1,\ldots,N\}$. The cooperative navigation problem is treated as a decentralized partially observable control problem. At decision time $t$, vessel $i$ obtains a local sensor observation, a goal-relative state, and its own kinematic state. It additionally receives learned messages from a bounded set of neighboring vessels. All vessels execute the same parameter-shared policy; therefore, the number of trainable policy parameters is independent of $N$.

Three neural modules are used: a \textit{MessageActor} $M_{\theta_M}$ that generates an outgoing message, a \textit{ControlActor} $\pi_{\theta_C}$ that generates rudder and thrust commands, and a Critic $V_{\theta_V}$ used during PPO training. Each module is COLREGs-conditioned and uses the shared-perception MoE described in Section~\ref{sec:shared_moe}. The policy is decentralized at execution: vessel $i$ requires only its own local quantities and messages received from nearby vessels.

The communication candidate set is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{N}_i(t)=\{j\neq i:d_{ij}(t)\le r_{\mathrm{comm}}\},
\end{equation}
and only the $K=4$ nearest members are retained. We denote this bounded set by $\mathcal{P}_i(t)$. In the reported experiments, $r_{\mathrm{comm}}=420$~m whereas the local radar range is $r_{\mathrm{radar}}=56$~m. Consequently, communication can provide information about vessels that are not yet represented by direct radar returns.

A route-level planner can optionally provide a sequence of waypoints, while the learned local policy treats the current waypoint as its goal. The A* integration in Section~\ref{sec:astar_result} is evaluated only after policy training and does not change the learned policy architecture.
}

\subsection{Observation Representation and Radar Encoder}
\label{sec:observation}
{\color{red}
The batched simulator maintains a 369-dimensional per-vessel observation
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{o}^{\mathrm{env}}_i=
[\mathbf{r}_i\|\mathbf{g}_i\|\mathbf{s}_i\|\mathbf{p}_i\|c_i]
\in\mathbb{R}^{369},
\label{eq:env_obs}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{r}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{360}$ is the raw $1^\circ$ radar scan, $\mathbf{g}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is the goal-relative observation, $\mathbf{s}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{4}$ is the self-state, $\mathbf{p}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is the absolute position used only to construct communication neighborhoods, and $c_i\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}$ is the COLREGs situation index. The absolute position is not supplied as a learned policy feature.

The goal observation is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{g}_i=
\left[
\frac{d_{g,i}}{d_{g,i}+150},\frac{\theta_{g,i}}{180^\circ}
\right],
\label{eq:goal_obs}
\end{equation}
where $d_{g,i}$ and $\theta_{g,i}$ are the distance and signed bearing to the current goal. The self-state is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s}_i=
\left[
\frac{v_i}{v_{\max,i}},
\frac{r_i}{r_{\max}},
\frac{\psi_i}{180^\circ},
\frac{\delta_i}{30^\circ}
\right].
\end{equation}

Radar is not reduced by a hand-crafted sector minimum. Instead, the latest three 360-ray scans are stacked as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R}_i(t)=
[\mathbf{r}_i(t-2),\mathbf{r}_i(t-1),\mathbf{r}_i(t)]
\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times360}.
\end{equation}
The three frames are treated as input channels to a learned RadarEncoder. Its computation is
\begin{align}
\mathbf{H}_1 &= \sigma\!\left(\mathrm{Conv}^{\mathrm{circ}}_{5,s=2}(\mathbf{R}_i);32\right),\\
\mathbf{H}_2 &= \sigma\!\left(\mathrm{Conv}^{\mathrm{circ}}_{5,s=2}(\mathbf{H}_1);64\right),\\
\mathbf{H}_3 &= \sigma\!\left(\mathrm{Conv}^{\mathrm{circ}}_{3,s=2}(\mathbf{H}_2);64\right),\\
\mathbf{z}^{r}_i &= \sigma\!\left(\mathbf{W}_r\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{H}_3)+\mathbf{b}_r\right)
\in\mathbb{R}^{30},
\label{eq:radar_encoder}
\end{align}
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is ReLU. The angular dimension is reduced as $360\rightarrow180\rightarrow90\rightarrow45$. Circular padding is used because rays $0^\circ$ and $359^\circ$ are adjacent in physical angle. Using the three temporal frames as channels lets the first convolutional layers respond to changes at the same angular bin, including bearing-rate information that would otherwise require a hand-engineered ARPA feature.

The encoded radar feature is concatenated with goal, self-state, and a five-dimensional one-hot representation of $c_i$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}_i=
[\mathbf{z}^{r}_i\|\mathbf{g}_i\|\mathbf{s}_i\|\operatorname{onehot}(c_i)]
\in\mathbb{R}^{41}.
\label{eq:core_input}
\end{equation}
Thus, 369 is the simulator observation dimension, not the dense-layer input dimension. The MessageActor receives the 41-dimensional representation in \eqref{eq:core_input}; the ControlActor and Critic additionally receive the six-dimensional aggregated communication feature, producing a 47-dimensional first dense-layer input.
}

\subsection{COLREGs Situation Assessment}
\label{sec:colregs_assessment}
{\color{red}
The situation index $c_i$ is not predicted by a neural classifier. It is obtained deterministically from pairwise vessel geometry and is used both as a policy input and as the hard-routing index of the MoE. The five classes are
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathrm{None},\mathrm{HeadOn},\mathrm{StandOn},\mathrm{GiveWay},\mathrm{Overtaking}\}.
\end{equation}

For each pair $(i,j)$ within the 56~m COLREGs detection range, let $\phi_{ij}$ denote the signed relative bearing of $j$ in the local frame of $i$, and let $\phi_{ji}$ denote the reciprocal bearing. The implementation applies the following cascade before assigning an active encounter class: vessels outside the detection range are discarded; vessels behind the own ship with $|\phi_{ij}|>100^\circ$ are discarded; pairs that are already passing safely port-to-port or starboard-to-starboard are discarded; and pairs with negative raw TCPA are discarded because the closest approach has already passed. Among the remaining pairs, mutual bearings within $15^\circ$ identify Head-On, a reciprocal bearing beyond $112.5^\circ$ identifies Overtaking, and the remaining region $|\phi_{ij}|<112.5^\circ$ is divided into GiveWay and StandOn according to crossing geometry. Importantly, there is no unused $5^\circ$ gap around the bow. For the near-axis region, the reciprocal bearing is used to prevent numerical switching between GiveWay and StandOn.

The pairwise urgency uses distance, TCPA, and DCPA:
\begin{align}
\mu_d(d_{ij}) &= \max\left(0,1-\frac{d_{ij}}{56}\right),\\
\mu_\tau(\tau_{ij}) &= \frac{1}{1+\tau_{ij}/30},\\
\mu_\delta(\delta_{ij}) &= \max\left(0,1-\frac{\delta_{ij}}{24}\right).
\end{align}
For an active encounter class $c_{ij}$, the risk score is
\begin{equation}
\rho_{ij}=
\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}
\left\{
\beta_{c_{ij}}
\left(0.3\mu_d+0.4\mu_\tau+0.3\mu_\delta\right)
\right\},
\label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\beta_{\mathrm{HeadOn}}=2.0,\quad
\beta_{\mathrm{GiveWay}}=1.5,\quad
\beta_{\mathrm{StandOn}}=1.3,\quad
\beta_{\mathrm{Overtaking}}=1.2.
\end{equation}
The dominant pair is
\begin{equation}
j_i^*=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{j\ne i}\rho_{ij},
\end{equation}
and $c_i=c_{ij_i^*}$; if no valid risk remains, $c_i=\mathrm{None}$. This deterministic index avoids the expert-collapse problem of a learned routing gate and keeps the routing rule identical during rollout and PPO update.
}

\subsection{Shared-Perception Mixture of Experts}
\label{sec:shared_moe}
{\color{red}
A naive situation-specific MoE duplicates the complete network for every COLREGs class. This is inefficient in the present architecture because the RadarEncoder dominates the parameter count. In the single-network policy, the three RadarEncoders used by the MessageActor, ControlActor, and Critic together contain 328,794 of 369,131 trainable parameters, approximately 89\%. Duplicating those encoders across five situations therefore increases model size without increasing the amount of data available to each situation.

The proposed architecture separates \emph{perception} from \emph{situation-specific decision making}. For each network $q\in\{M,C,V\}$, one RadarEncoder $E^{(q)}$ is shared by its five experts, whereas the downstream core $F^{(q)}_k$ is specific to situation $k$. The three networks do not share a single encoder with one another; sharing occurs across the five experts within each network. The computation can be written as
\begin{align}
\mathbf{z}^{(q)}_i &= E^{(q)}(\mathbf{R}_i),\\
\mathbf{h}^{(q)}_i &= F^{(q)}_{c_i}
\left(
[\mathbf{z}^{(q)}_i\|\mathbf{g}_i\|\mathbf{s}_i\|\operatorname{onehot}(c_i)\|\mathbf{q}^{(q)}_i]
\right),
\label{eq:moe_route}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{q}^{(M)}_i$ is empty and $\mathbf{q}^{(C)}_i=\mathbf{q}^{(V)}_i=\widehat{\mathbf{m}}_i$ are the gated communication features defined in Section~\ref{sec:message_passing}. Only the selected core receives the sample and its gradient.

For the MessageActor,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m}_i=	anh\!\left(\mathbf{W}^{M}_{c_i}\mathbf{h}^{(M)}_i+\mathbf{b}^{M}_{c_i}\right)
\in[-1,1]^6.
\label{eq:message}
\end{equation}
The ControlActor uses a 128-unit situation-specific hidden layer followed by a 64-unit layer and a two-dimensional action head. The Critic uses the corresponding situation-specific value core. The policy action is sampled by a squashed Gaussian:
\begin{align}
\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_i&\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i,\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2)),\\
\mathbf{a}_i&=\tanh(\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_i),
\end{align}
where the two components correspond to normalized rudder and thrust commands. The log probability includes the standard $\tanh$ Jacobian correction.

Table~\ref{tab:moe_arch} summarizes the four structures used to isolate parameter capacity and expert separation.

\subsection{Position-Grounded Multi-Neighbor Message Passing}
\label{sec:message_passing}
{\color{red}
A six-dimensional latent message alone does not identify where its sender is located relative to the receiver. Rather than requiring the MessageActor to spend part of its six-dimensional channel on absolute or relative position, the receiver computes that geometry locally. For each $j\in\mathcal{P}_i$, the relative-position feature is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}_{ij}=
\left[
\sin\phi_{ij},\cos\phi_{ij},\frac{d_{ij}}{420}
\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.
\label{eq:relpos}
\end{equation}
It is concatenated with the sender message and transformed by a shared localization encoder,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_{ij}=f_{\mathrm{loc}}
\left([\mathbf{r}_{ij}\|\mathbf{m}_j]\right),
\qquad
f_{\mathrm{loc}}:\mathbb{R}^{9}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{32}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{6}.
\label{eq:msg_encoder}
\end{equation}
The receiver then uses a masked mean over at most four partners:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\mathbf{m}}_i=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_i|}
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_i}\mathbf{e}_{ij}, & |\mathcal{P}_i|>0,\\[8pt]
\mathbf{0}, & |\mathcal{P}_i|=0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:message_aggregation}
\end{equation}
This aggregation is permutation invariant and preserves the spatial attribution of each message before the averaging operation.

The ControlActor and Critic each contain a learnable scalar message gate in every active decision core. The communication feature supplied to the core is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf{m}}_i=\sigma(g_{c_i})\bar{\mathbf{m}}_i,
\label{eq:message_gate}
\end{equation}
where $g_{c_i}$ is initialized to zero, so $\sigma(g_{c_i})=0.5$. No gate-opening penalty is used. The policy can therefore reduce the effect of unhelpful messages rather than being forced to consume them. A separate gate is used in the Critic so that noisy communication does not necessarily perturb the value baseline in the same way as the actor.
}

\subsection{Communication Learning and Credit Assignment}
\label{sec:comm_learning}
{\color{red}
Two implementation details are required for the learned communication channel to receive a useful gradient.

\textit{First, the communication path is initialized with small but nonzero weights.} If the message-producing layer and the message-consuming slice are both initialized to zero, the communication path contains products of the form $ab$ with $a=b=0$, yielding zero derivative with respect to both sides. This zero-gradient saddle was observed to keep the channel bit-wise unchanged during training. The MessageActor output weights and the message-consuming weights are therefore initialized at one tenth of their standard magnitude, while the message gate is initialized at its neutral value in \eqref{eq:message_gate}.

\textit{Second, sender-to-receiver credit assignment is explicitly preserved during PPO updates.} A receiver's action at rollout time depends on messages generated from partner observations. Treating those messages as detached rollout constants would optimize the receiver but provide no policy-gradient signal to the partner MessageActor. The rollout buffer therefore stores, for every receiver, the selected partner radar stacks, goal states, self states, relative positions, validity masks, and COLREGs situation indices. During the PPO update, partner messages are regenerated by $M_{\theta_M}$ and re-aggregated using exactly Eqs.~\eqref{eq:relpos}--\eqref{eq:message_aggregation}. Consequently, the receiver's PPO loss differentiates through $\bar{\mathbf{m}}_i$ into the parameters that generated $\mathbf{m}_j$.

The same requirement applies to the action likelihood. The rollout stores the pre-$\tanh$ action sample $\widetilde{\mathbf{a}}_i$ rather than numerically reconstructing it from a saturated action. Reusing the stored sample during the update makes the rollout and update log probabilities identical before any optimizer step and prevents an artificial PPO ratio error caused by inverse-$\tanh$ clipping.

A self-supervised ThreatDecoder further regularizes message content. For each sender, the three nearest detected radar threats are represented as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y}_{i,k}^{\mathrm{th}}=
[\sin\varphi_{i,k},\cos\varphi_{i,k},\bar d_{i,k},\dot{\bar d}_{i,k}],
\qquad k=1,2,3,
\end{equation}
where $\bar d$ is normalized radar distance and $\dot{\bar d}$ is estimated from the two most recent radar frames. A decoder
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{th}}:\mathbb{R}^{6}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{64}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{12}
\end{equation}
predicts these three four-dimensional target vectors from the sender message. Missing threat slots are masked. The masked $L_1$ loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{th}}=
\frac{\sum_{i,k,q}M_{i,k,q}
|\widehat y^{\mathrm{th}}_{i,k,q}-y^{\mathrm{th}}_{i,k,q}|}
{\sum_{i,k,q}M_{i,k,q}+\epsilon}.
\label{eq:threat_loss}
\end{equation}
The reported experiments use weight $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}=0.5$. This loss acts through the MessageActor and its decoder but does not directly supervise the receiving ControlActor, preserving the receiver's freedom to use or ignore the channel. The implementation also supports intent, goal, role, and consumer-side auxiliary decoders; their coefficients are zero in the experimental configuration reported here.
}

Dense coefficients are applied at the simulator's physical-step resolution and accumulated over the ten physical substeps between policy decisions.

The COLREGs term is a directional noncompliance penalty rather than a generic positive compliance score. Let positive $a_i^\delta$ denote a starboard command. Then
\begin{align}
R_{i}^{\mathrm{COLREGs}}={}&-\alpha_c(1+\rho_i)
\mathbf{1}[\rho_i>0.3]
\Big(
\mathbf{1}[c_i\in\{\mathrm{HeadOn},\mathrm{GiveWay},\mathrm{Overtaking}\}]
\max(-a_i^\delta,0)
\nonumber\\
&\hspace{35mm}+
\mathbf{1}[c_i=\mathrm{StandOn}]|a_i^\delta|
\Big),
\label{eq:colregs_penalty}
\end{align}
with $\alpha_c=0.45$. Thus, Head-On, GiveWay, and Overtaking encounters penalize port-side steering while leaving a starboard maneuver unpenalized, whereas StandOn penalizes any steering command and therefore favors course keeping. The term becomes active only when the dominant pairwise risk exceeds 0.3. This asymmetry is important because it resolves the symmetric failure mode in which two independently cautious agents choose incompatible avoidance directions.

The Standard checkpoints terminate an episode on arrival, collision, or timeout. No spinning termination or spinning reward is used. The maximum episode duration is 16,000 physics steps, corresponding to 1,600 policy decisions.
}

\subsection{PPO Objective and Two-Stage Communication Curriculum}
\label{sec:training}
{\color{red}
The policy is optimized by PPO with generalized advantage estimation. For a minibatch $\mathcal{B}$, the objective minimized by the implementation is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PPO}}
+\alpha_v\mathcal{L}_{V}
-\alpha_e\mathcal{H}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{th}}
+\lambda_m\mathcal{L}_{m},
\label{eq:ppo_total}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_v=0.5$, $\alpha_e=0.01$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}=0.5$, and $\mathcal{L}_{m}$ is a small message-magnitude regularizer. The PPO clip parameter is 0.2, $\gamma=0.99$, GAE parameter is 0.95, learning rate is $3\times10^{-4}$, two PPO epochs are used per rollout, the minibatch size is 512, and the gradient norm is clipped at 0.5.

The principal training runs use a two-stage communication curriculum within one 16-million-decision run. During the first stage, incoming communication is identically zero and the shared policy learns goal-directed navigation and collision avoidance from local sensing. At 9 million decisions, learned communication is activated and remains active for the rest of training. The model parameters are continuous across this transition; the second stage is not a separately initialized model. The COLREGs coefficient remains $0.45$ throughout both stages. This distinction is important: the curriculum changes only \emph{when communication becomes available}; it does not introduce a third stage with a different COLREGs weight.

The delayed-communication design is explicitly tested against communication active from the first training step in Section~\ref{sec:timing_result}. A separate communication-OFF arm is trained for the full budget to measure the value of communication itself. These two comparisons answer different questions: OFF versus ON measures whether communication helps, whereas communication-from-start versus delayed communication measures whether staging the communication channel improves optimization.
}

\subsection{GPU-Batched Maritime Simulator}
{\color{red}
The experiments are trained and evaluated in a GPU-batched simulator, \texttt{vessel\_gym.py}, that reproduces the vessel dynamics, radar sensing, collision geometry, COLREGs situation logic, and observation contract of the Unity/C\# reference environment. This removes the synchronous Unity--Python throughput bottleneck while retaining the same neural policy modules and checkpoint format. \subsection{Controlled Comparison Design}
{\color{red}
Each primary experiment changes one axis while keeping the remaining configuration fixed. Communication ON/OFF changes only message availability; the aggregation experiment changes only the number of retained communication partners; the message-dimensionality experiment changes only the latent message width; the COLREGs experiment changes only the COLREGs shaping coefficient; and the curriculum experiment changes only the communication activation time. The MoE comparison changes the network decomposition by construction and explicitly reports model size.

The reported bar metrics are not taken from noisy training windows. After training, each checkpoint is frozen and evaluated by repeated navigation rollouts. The standard evaluator uses 96 parallel environments, a 1,500-decision burn-in period to decorrelate simultaneous episode starts, and 3,500 subsequent evaluation decisions. Metrics include arrival, collision and timeout rates, fuel/control-effort accumulation, total heading travel, minimum vessel separation, episode length, and overall and situation-specific COLREGs compliance.

Learning curves and fixed-policy bars use different estimators and therefore are not compared numerically. For visualization, the learning log is converted to an outcome score
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{curve}}=
1.5P_{\mathrm{goal}}-6.0P_{\mathrm{collision}}-0.5P_{\mathrm{timeout}},
\label{eq:curve_score}
\end{equation}
weighted by the number of terminated episodes in each window. The raw dense training reward is not used for this curve because its measured correlation with final episode outcome was approximately zero in the logged runs. Changing the collision coefficient over a broad range did not change the ordering of the compared conditions.

Unless explicitly stated otherwise, results are averaged over seeds 42--44. The two known exceptions are disclosed in the corresponding subsections: the parameter-matched thin MoE currently has one completed seed, and the original message-dimension sweep was performed with the fully separated full-width MoE rather than the proposed shared-perception architecture.
}

\subsection{Does Shared Perception Improve the MoE Design?}
\label{sec:moe_result}
{\color{red}
The MoE comparison is constructed to separate expert specialization from parameter capacity. A single network contains 369,131 parameters. A thin fully separated MoE is width-scaled to 363,004 parameters, so it is approximately capacity matched to the single network. A full-width separated MoE contains 1,826,719 parameters because the RadarEncoder is replicated five times in each of the three networks. The proposed shared-perception MoE contains 511,543 parameters.

The delivered evaluations show a clear safety advantage for shared perception. The single network reaches 57.2\% arrival but has a 6.07\% collision rate. The thin separated model degrades substantially, with 33.8\% arrival and 9.50\% collision. The proposed shared-perception model attains 55.6\% arrival with only 1.83\% collision, while also reducing fuel and heading travel relative to the single and thin models. The thin model's high 66.1\% aggregate COLREGs compliance should not be interpreted as superior navigation because it co-occurs with very low arrival and high collision; the policy produces fewer successful, informative encounters.

The full-width separated arm is necessary to isolate whether sharing, rather than model capacity, causes the safety gain. Existing full-width runs provide this comparison without new training and are being incorporated into the final four-arm figure. The full-width model uses about 3.6 times as many parameters as the proposed model. Its inclusion makes the causal comparison explicit: the proposed model does not simply win by allocating more capacity than a baseline; it retains full-width perception while preventing that expensive perception module from being partitioned into five independently trained copies.

The thin separated condition currently has one seed, whereas the single and proposed conditions have three. This imbalance is reported in the figure caption and is not used for a precise effect-size claim. One single-network seed also converges to an unusually aggressive policy with much higher collision rate, which explains the larger uncertainty of that condition.
}

\subsection{Effect of COLREGs Shaping}
\label{sec:colregs_result}
{\color{red}
The COLREGs experiment changes only the coefficient in Eq.~\eqref{eq:colregs_penalty} from 0 to 0.45. The original plotted comparison used a full-width separated network and only one seed for the no-COLREGs arm. A matched three-seed re-evaluation using the proposed shared-perception architecture has already been completed and should be used in the final figure. In that matched comparison, the COLREGs term raises compliance from 31.4\% to 52.3\% and reduces collision rate from 6.43\% to 1.83\%. All three seeds show the same direction for both metrics.

The mechanism is more informative than the direct compliance increase. Eq.~\eqref{eq:colregs_penalty} breaks a symmetry in collision avoidance: a Head-On, GiveWay, or Overtaking agent is specifically discouraged from turning to port, while a StandOn agent is discouraged from changing course. This common convention reduces the chance that two independently cautious policies choose incompatible maneuvers. The accompanying collision reduction therefore shows that the COLREGs term functions not only as a rule-compliance score but also as a coordination prior.

\subsection{When Should Communication Be Introduced?}
\label{sec:timing_result}
{\color{red}
The communication-timing comparison distinguishes optimization schedule from communication value. Both arms use communication by the end of training and both are trained for the full 16-million-decision budget. The first activates communication from the initial step; the second keeps incoming messages zero until 9 million decisions and then activates the same communication mechanism.

Delayed activation produces the higher learning-curve score (2.12 versus 1.06), lower collision rate (1.83\% versus 6.53\%), and higher COLREGs compliance (52.3\% versus 46.4\%). The only aggregate metric favoring communication from the start is arrival rate, 61.3\% versus 55.6\%. This mean is unstable across seeds: the arrival-rate spread is approximately $\pm14.5$ percentage points for communication from the start but only $\pm1.4$ for delayed activation. The high-arrival seed also adopts a more aggressive policy with substantially more collisions and fewer timeouts. Consequently, the timing result should be interpreted as a safety--stability result rather than as a claim that every single metric improves under delayed communication.

The learning curve also supplies a useful diagnostic unavailable in a pure ON/OFF comparison: the two schedules differ in communication availability at a known point in training. The performance separation after communication becomes active is therefore directly associated with whether the local navigation policy was established before the channel had to be interpreted.
}

\subsection{Heterogeneous Communication Capability}
\label{sec:mixed_result}
{\color{red}
A mixed-fleet evaluation holds the trained proposed policy fixed and disables transmission for 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, or 14 of the 16 vessels. These vessels remain able to receive messages (the \texttt{rx} condition), so the experiment isolates loss of broadcasting rather than complete sensor failure. Non-transmitting vessels are distributed around the spawn ring rather than clustered spatially. Each plotted fleet metric is a sample-count-weighted aggregate because the appropriate denominator differs across metrics.

The currently delivered figure suggests that aggregate COLREGs compliance remains within 51.4--52.3\% and collision rate within 1.08--2.60\% over the seven fleet compositions, indicating substantial robustness to missing transmitters. This result is not used as a primary quantitative claim in the present revision because the archived raw-data bundle does not yet reproduce the delivered figure and contains a different seed count. \subsection{Integration with Global A* Guidance}
\label{sec:astar_result}
{\color{red}
The local policy can also be coupled to a route-level A* planner by replacing the direct destination with a sequence of collision-free waypoints. The batched evaluator reproduces the Unity waypoint pipeline using grid A*, line-of-sight string pulling, and minimum-spacing waypoint merging. The final waypoint remains the true destination; intermediate waypoints do not trigger episode completion.

The route generator has been exhaustively checked over 20 spawn points and 16 goals. Across all 320 combinations, no path intersects the inflated obstacle region or the physical collision radius, and the final waypoint equals the intended goal in all 320 cases. The path-to-straight-line ratio has median 1.000 and maximum 1.074. A separate real-coastline demonstration constructs the occupancy grid from Natural Earth land polygons and computes an A* route between the Korean peninsula and Taiwan; this map demonstrates route generation and is not an AIS-driven traffic digital twin.

A direct-versus-A* navigation-performance claim requires one additional controlled evaluation. The previous A* harness used a different ring default and a checkpoint from the weaker communication-from-start arm. The final comparison should instead use the proposed checkpoint, $\mathrm{ring}=0.7$, and report both all 320 spawn--goal pairs and the 148 pairs for which the straight path is actually blocked. The latter subset is necessary because 172 of the 320 pairs already have a clear direct line and therefore produce identical direct and A* guidance. In addition, the current policy was trained with direct goals rather than waypoint following, so the A* test is explicitly a zero-shot integration unless waypoint-conditioned retraining is performed.

\section{Conclusion}
{\color{red}
This paper developed a communication-enabled cooperative navigation framework for multi-vessel autonomous navigation under partial observability. The implemented method combines three elements that must operate consistently: learned inter-vessel messages, deterministic COLREGs-conditioned expert routing, and a policy-gradient path that allows the receiving vessel's navigation objective to train the transmitting vessel's MessageActor.

The central architectural result is that situation specialization should not require situation-specific perception. A learned circular-convolution RadarEncoder accounts for approximately 89\% of the single-network parameter count. Fully separating this encoder across five COLREGs experts increases the complete model to 1.83 million parameters and fragments the perception data available to each expert. Sharing perception across the five hard-routed decision cores reduces the model to approximately 0.51 million parameters while achieving a collision rate of 1.83\% in the common 16-vessel evaluation. The communication mechanism further preserves the physical origin of each compact message by attaching receiver-computed relative position before masked-mean aggregation.

Controlled comparisons show that communication reduces collision rate from 2.77\% to 1.83\% while improving aggregate COLREGs compliance and reducing fuel and heading travel. Aggregating four neighbors rather than only the nearest one reduces collision from 3.43\% to 1.83\%. A matched COLREGs-shaping comparison shows that the directional rule term reduces collision from 6.43\% to 1.83\% while increasing compliance from 31.4\% to 52.3\%. Finally, introducing communication after an initial local-navigation stage yields substantially lower collision and lower inter-seed variability than exposing the policy to the communication channel from the first step.

The current framework abstracts communication as range-limited message exchange and evaluates global A* guidance as an external waypoint layer. Future work will incorporate communication latency, loss and bandwidth constraints, train explicitly with global waypoint sequences, and evaluate the resulting system using traffic data and higher-fidelity maritime simulators. These extensions will allow the learned local coordination mechanism to be assessed under heterogeneous vessel dynamics and realistic operational communication conditions.
}
