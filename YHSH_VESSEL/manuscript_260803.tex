\begin{abstract}
{\color{violet}
Cooperative navigation among Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS) is difficult in partially observable maritime environments because each vessel must make collision-avoidance decisions from incomplete local information while simultaneously satisfying encounter-dependent COLREGs obligations. This paper develops a communication-enabled cooperative navigation framework based on a simple design principle: information that is common across encounter types is shared, behavior that differs across COLREGs obligations is specialized, and information missing from local sensing is exchanged among vessels. Each vessel encodes its local observation into a compact latent message. At the receiver, the message is combined with relative bearing and distance before multi-neighbor aggregation so that learned semantic information remains associated with its physical origin. A COLREGs-conditioned Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) shares the radar perception module across encounter experts while retaining situation-specific decision cores. Communication is introduced after an initial local-navigation stage, and an auxiliary threat-decoding objective encourages transmitted messages to preserve collision-relevant information observed by the sender.

The framework is evaluated in a GPU-batched maritime simulator with 16 simultaneously navigating vessels. Controlled experiments separately examine the necessity of communication, shared-perception expert design, multi-neighbor aggregation, message dimensionality, directional COLREGs shaping, and communication-introduction timing. Communication reduces collision rate while improving COLREGs compliance and navigation efficiency. The shared-perception MoE provides a favorable safety--model-size tradeoff relative to a single network and fully separated experts, and aggregating several spatially grounded messages is substantially safer than using only the nearest neighbor. Additional experiments examine heterogeneous communication capability and integration with an external A* route planner. The results show that communication-enabled distributed intelligence can improve cooperative MASS navigation while retaining decentralized execution.
}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
{\color{violet}
Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS) must navigate toward their destinations while avoiding static obstacles and surrounding vessels under the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs). This problem becomes particularly difficult in dense traffic because the surrounding vessels are not independent obstacles: the maneuver selected by one vessel changes the encounter geometry observed by the others. Reviews of autonomous maritime navigation therefore identify multi-vessel interaction, partial observability, and COLREGs-compliant decision making as central challenges for practical autonomy \citep{Thombre,Complexity,Sarhadi}.

Conventional global planners such as A* and Dijkstra's algorithm are effective for static route generation, while local methods such as velocity-obstacle approaches, artificial potential fields, and model-predictive control react to short-horizon collision constraints \citep{Yuan,58}. Learning-based methods instead acquire navigation behavior through repeated interaction with simulated environments, and several studies have incorporated COLREGs constraints into deep reinforcement learning policies \citep{Zhao-COLREGs}. Most of these methods, however, still treat surrounding vessels as objects to be observed rather than agents that can exchange information and coordinate their behavior.

Cooperative navigation addresses this limitation by allowing vessels to share information that is locally available to one vessel but unavailable to another. Existing maritime cooperation studies have considered explicit state exchange, control intentions, historical trajectories, and learned latent communication \citep{Akdag,chen,82,29}. Recent multi-agent reinforcement learning has demonstrated that learned inter-vessel communication can improve collision avoidance \citep{WangZhao2025}. Extending this idea beyond simple pairwise encounters introduces three practical design questions. First, a vessel can receive messages from several neighbors at once, so a variable-size message set must be transformed into a fixed-dimensional policy input. Second, the same physical scene can imply different control obligations under Head-On, Give-Way, Stand-On, and Overtaking situations. Third, a learned communication channel is useful only when the receiving vessel's navigation objective can assign credit to the message generated by the transmitting vessel.

These observations lead to the design principle used throughout this paper: \emph{share what is common, specialize what is behaviorally different, and communicate what is locally missing}. Radar perception is a common physical inference problem and is therefore shared across encounter experts. The control response differs by COLREGs obligation and is therefore specialized. Information outside the local sensing horizon is supplied through inter-vessel communication. At the receiver, learned message content is grounded by relative bearing and distance before aggregation, separating \emph{what} the sender communicates from \emph{where} that information originates.

The proposed framework combines four components. First, a three-frame circular-convolution radar encoder produces a compact perception feature. Second, deterministic COLREGs geometry hard-routes the feature to a situation-specific decision core while sharing the radar encoder across experts. Third, each sender produces a six-dimensional latent message, and each receiver combines up to four spatially grounded messages using permutation-invariant aggregation. Fourth, partner messages are recomputed during PPO updates so that the receiver's policy loss remains differentiable with respect to the sender's MessageActor. A self-supervised ThreatDecoder additionally encourages the latent channel to retain collision-relevant information.

The main contributions are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Position-grounded multi-neighbor communication:} the receiver combines a compact learned message with sender-relative bearing and distance before aggregation, preserving spatial attribution without requiring the transmitted message itself to encode position.

    \item \textbf{Shared-perception, situation-specific decision making:} deterministic COLREGs routing selects one of five decision cores, while the parameter-dominant radar encoder is shared across them. This avoids unnecessary replication of perception while preserving encounter-specific control behavior.

    \item \textbf{Communication learning with explicit credit assignment:} partner observations are retained in the rollout buffer and partner messages are regenerated during PPO updates, allowing the receiver's control loss to train the sender's MessageActor. A threat-decoding auxiliary objective provides an additional representation-learning signal.

    \item \textbf{Component-wise experimental validation:} the experiments ask one design question at a time---whether communication is useful, where expert specialization should occur, how many neighbors should be aggregated, how wide the message channel should be, how COLREGs knowledge should be injected, and when communication should be introduced during training.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~2 introduces the vessel model and COLREGs background. Section~3 reviews path planning and cooperative MASS navigation. Section~4 presents the proposed framework. Section~5 describes the simulator and evaluation protocol. Section~6 presents the experimental results, including the eight component-wise and system-integration studies. Section~7 concludes the paper.
}

This section summarizes the vessel motion model and the COLREGs encounter types considered in the cooperative navigation problem.
}

\subsection{Vessel Dynamics}
{\color{violet}
Fig.~\ref{fig:vessel_model} illustrates the vessel control variables. The vessel pose in the earth-fixed frame is $\boldsymbol{\eta}=[x,y,\psi]^T$, where $x$ and $y$ are position and $\psi$ is heading. The body-fixed velocity is $\boldsymbol{v}=[u,v,r]^T$, where $u$, $v$, and $r$ denote surge, sway, and yaw rate, respectively. The standard planar kinematic relation is
\begin{equation}
\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=\mathbf{T}(\psi)\boldsymbol{v},
\label{eq:kinematics}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{T}(\psi)$ rotates body-frame velocity into the earth-fixed frame. The corresponding vessel dynamics are conventionally written as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}+\mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}+\mathbf{D}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}+\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\mathbf{B}\boldsymbol{\tau},
\label{eq:dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ contains the propulsion and rudder inputs. The simulator used in this work restricts motion to the horizontal plane and implements the corresponding surge and yaw response through acceleration, deceleration, drag, and rudder-response parameters. The learned policy therefore controls two normalized commands: thrust and rudder.
}

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figure/png/fig2}
    \caption{Principal COLREGs encounter situations considered in this study.}
    \label{fig:colregs_background}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Collision Avoidance and COLREGs}
{\color{violet}
COLREGs specify different obligations according to encounter geometry. Fig.~\ref{fig:colregs_background} illustrates the encounter types used in this study. In a Head-On encounter, both vessels alter course to starboard. In a crossing encounter, the Give-Way vessel takes early action to keep clear, whereas the Stand-On vessel should maintain course and speed unless collision avoidance requires intervention. In an Overtaking encounter, the overtaking vessel keeps clear until it is finally past and clear \citep{Zhao-COLREGs,Complexity}.

A multi-vessel encounter is more difficult than an isolated pairwise encounter because the same vessel can hold different obligations relative to different neighbors. Collision urgency is therefore evaluated from pairwise geometry using current distance, Time to Closest Point of Approach (TCPA), and Distance at Closest Point of Approach (DCPA). These quantities determine the dominant encounter used by the policy routing and the COLREGs shaping term described in Section~\ref{sec:colregs_routing}.
}

\section{Related Work}
% =====================================================================
{\color{violet}
\subsection{Path Planning for MASS}
Global path planning determines a route or waypoint sequence between origin and destination, whereas local path planning resolves dynamic collision constraints during execution \citep{Yuan}. Search-based methods such as A* are attractive for static occupancy maps because they provide a clear route-level objective. Data-driven global planners have also exploited historical AIS trajectories and environmental information to improve waypoint generation and voyage efficiency \citep{119,120}. These methods are complementary to local cooperative control: a route planner determines where the vessel should go, while the local controller must decide how to move safely among other vessels.

Local collision avoidance has been addressed with velocity-obstacle methods, artificial potential fields, model predictive control, rule-based methods, and reinforcement learning \citep{58,Sarhadi}. Reinforcement learning is particularly useful when the mapping from multi-vessel observations to control actions is difficult to specify analytically. COLREGs-compliant DRL has demonstrated that encounter-dependent maritime rules can be incorporated into learned collision avoidance \citep{Zhao-COLREGs}. However, most local DRL formulations still operate from the own ship's observation alone.

\subsection{Cooperative MASS Navigation}
Cooperative navigation supplements local sensing by exchanging information among vessels. Prior work has considered shared position and risk information, intended control actions, negotiation protocols, and historical trajectories \citep{Akdag,chen,82,29}. Learned communication replaces hand-designed message contents with a compact latent representation optimized jointly with the navigation policy. \citet{WangZhao2025}, for example, demonstrated communication-enabled cooperative ship navigation in a small multi-agent setting.

Scaling learned communication to dense encounters introduces two representation problems. The number of neighbors varies, so direct concatenation produces a variable-dimensional input. In addition, a latent message without sender geometry can lose physical meaning after aggregation. The present work therefore grounds each message by receiver-computed relative geometry before permutation-invariant aggregation. A separate issue is situation specialization. Rather than learning a soft gate, the proposed MoE uses the COLREGs situation already defined by maritime geometry as a hard routing variable. This avoids spending model capacity and training data on rediscovering a known encounter partition.
}

\section{Proposed Framework}
\label{sec:framework}
% =====================================================================
{\color{red}
The proposed algorithm separates three roles that are often mixed together in cooperative navigation: perception of the physical scene, decision making under a COLREGs obligation, and communication of information unavailable to another vessel. The first is shared, the second is specialized, and the third is learned jointly with control.
}

\subsection{System Overview}
\label{sec:system_overview}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.92\textwidth]{figure/fig6}
    \caption{Overall cooperative-navigation framework. Each vessel forms a local observation, determines the active COLREGs context, generates a compact message for neighboring vessels, aggregates received information, and produces continuous navigation controls. The final implementation uses deterministic COLREGs hard routing and the shared-perception MoE described in Section~\ref{sec:shared_moe}.}
    \label{fig:framework_structure}
\end{figure*}
% TODO(FIGURE-LABEL): if the existing artwork still shows a learned/fuzzy gate or old dimensions, update only those labels; retain the reusable layout.

{\color{red}
Consider $N$ vessels indexed by $i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}$. Each vessel executes the same parameter-shared policy and acts from its local observation and messages received from nearby vessels. The execution architecture consists of a MessageActor $M_{\theta_M}$, a ControlActor $\pi_{\theta_C}$, and a Critic $V_{\theta_V}$ used during PPO training. Fig.~\ref{fig:framework_structure} shows the information flow.

The environment observation is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{o}^{\mathrm{env}}_i=[\mathbf{r}_i\|\mathbf{g}_i\|\mathbf{s}_i\|\mathbf{p}_i\|c_i]\in\mathbb{R}^{369},
\label{eq:env_obs}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{r}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{360}$ is the radar scan, $\mathbf{g}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is the goal-relative state, $\mathbf{s}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{4}$ is the vessel self-state, $\mathbf{p}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ is position, and $c_i$ is the COLREGs situation index. Position is used to construct communication geometry but is not supplied as a direct learned policy feature.
}

\subsection{Shared Radar Perception}
\label{sec:perception}
{\color{red}
The latest three 360-ray scans are stacked and processed by a circular one-dimensional convolutional encoder,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}^{r}_i=E_r\!\left(\mathbf{R}_i\right),
\qquad
\mathbf{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times360},
\label{eq:radar_simple}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}^{r}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{30}$. The encoder uses three stride-2 convolutional layers with output channels 32, 64, and 64, followed by a fully connected projection to 30 dimensions. Circular padding reflects the physical adjacency of the $0^\circ$ and $359^\circ$ rays.

The goal distance is normalized as $d_g/(d_g+150)$ and the goal angle by $180^\circ$. The learned local feature supplied to the decision network is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_i=[\mathbf{z}^{r}_i\|\mathbf{g}_i\|\mathbf{s}_i\|\operatorname{onehot}(c_i)]\in\mathbb{R}^{41}.
\label{eq:local_feature}
\end{equation}
Thus, the raw simulator observation dimension and the dense policy-input dimension are intentionally different: the high-dimensional radar is first converted into a compact learned perception feature.
}

\subsection{COLREGs-Conditioned Shared-Perception MoE}
\label{sec:colregs_routing}
\label{sec:shared_moe}
{\color{red}
The situation index $c_i$ is obtained from deterministic pairwise encounter geometry rather than a learned gating network. Vessels outside the 56~m COLREGs detection range, vessels clearly behind the own ship, pairs that have already passed, and pairs with negative TCPA are excluded. The remaining geometry is classified into None, Head-On, Stand-On, Give-Way, or Overtaking. When several active pairs are present, the dominant situation is the class of the highest-risk pair.

For pair $(i,j)$, the risk score combines normalized distance, TCPA, and DCPA contributions,
\begin{equation}
\rho_{ij}=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\left[
\beta_{c_{ij}}\left(0.3r_d+0.4r_{\tau}+0.3r_{\delta}\right)
\right],
\label{eq:risk_simple}
\end{equation}
where the encounter multipliers are 2.0 for Head-On, 1.5 for Give-Way, 1.3 for Stand-On, and 1.2 for Overtaking. The dominant situation $c_i$ is selected from the pair with the largest $\rho_{ij}$.

The key architectural choice is not to duplicate the entire network for every situation. For each of the MessageActor, ControlActor, and Critic, the radar perception module is shared across the five experts while the downstream decision core is situation specific. In compact form,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_i=F_{c_i}\!\left([E_r(\mathbf{R}_i)\|\mathbf{g}_i\|\mathbf{s}_i\|\mathbf{q}_i]\right),
\label{eq:shared_moe_simple}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{q}_i$ is empty for message generation and contains the aggregated communication feature for control and value estimation. This design follows the interpretation that the physical scene is common across COLREGs situations, whereas the appropriate response differs by obligation.

The reason for sharing perception is also practical. Across the MessageActor, ControlActor, and Critic, the radar encoders account for 328,794 of the 369,131 parameters in the single-network implementation. Fully duplicating perception across five experts therefore creates a large parameter increase while splitting perception training data among situations. The architecture comparison in Section~\ref{sec:moe_result} directly tests this design choice.
}

\subsection{Position-Grounded Multi-Neighbor Communication}
\label{sec:message_passing}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.90\textwidth]{figure/fig7}
    \caption{Message-passing architecture. A transmitting vessel generates a compact latent message from its local features. At the receiver, sender-relative geometry is attached before the messages are combined and supplied to the ControlActor. The implemented aggregation is the position-grounded masked mean in Eq.~\eqref{eq:message_aggregation}.}
    \label{fig:message_architecture}
\end{figure*}
% TODO(FIGURE-LABEL): retain the existing flow diagram, but replace any old "sum" label by position-grounded masked mean and update old observation dimensions if still present.

{\color{red}
The MessageActor converts the local feature into a six-dimensional latent vector,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m}_i=M_{\theta_M}(\mathbf{x}_i)\in[-1,1]^6.
\label{eq:message_simple}
\end{equation}
The message represents \emph{what} the sender has learned from its local observation. It is not required to encode its own position. Instead, the receiver computes \emph{where} the sender is located. For sender $j$ and receiver $i$, the relative geometry is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}_{ij}=\left[\sin\phi_{ij},\cos\phi_{ij},d_{ij}/r_{\mathrm{comm}}\right].
\label{eq:relpos_simple}
\end{equation}
The receiver then forms a spatially grounded message
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_{ij}=f_{\mathrm{msg}}([\mathbf{m}_j\|\mathbf{r}_{ij}]),
\label{eq:grounded_msg}
\end{equation}
where $f_{\mathrm{msg}}$ is a small shared MLP. Among vessels within the 420~m communication range, the nearest $K=4$ are retained. Their grounded messages are combined by
\begin{equation}
\bar{\mathbf{m}}_i=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_i|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_i}\mathbf{e}_{ij},
\label{eq:message_aggregation}
\end{equation}
with $\bar{\mathbf{m}}_i=\mathbf{0}$ when no partner is available. The masked mean is permutation invariant, while the relative geometry is injected before averaging so that spatial context is not discarded by the aggregation operation.

The ControlActor and Critic use a learnable scalar gate to scale the aggregated message before it enters the selected expert core. The gate is initialized to a neutral value rather than being forced open. Communication can therefore be suppressed when it is not useful; this is a representational property of the model, not a guarantee that finite training always improves performance.
}

\subsection{Communication Learning and Threat Decoding}
\label{sec:comm_learning}
{\color{red}
The communication path must remain part of the PPO computation graph. During rollout, the buffer therefore stores the observations of the partners that contributed to each receiver. During the PPO update, partner messages are regenerated by the current MessageActor and aggregated again using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:relpos_simple}--\eqref{eq:message_aggregation}. As a result, a receiver's policy loss can update the parameters that generated its neighbors' messages rather than treating those messages as fixed rollout data.

A self-supervised ThreatDecoder regularizes message content. For each sender, the three nearest radar threats are represented by bearing, normalized distance, and closing information. A decoder predicts these threat descriptors from the six-dimensional message,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{th}}_i=D_{\mathrm{th}}(\mathbf{m}_i),
\end{equation}
and a masked $L_1$ loss is applied only to valid threat slots. The reported experiments use threat-decoder weight $0.5$. This objective encourages the message to retain locally observed collision information, but it does not directly prescribe how the receiving ControlActor must respond to that information.
}

\subsection{Reward and PPO Training}
\label{sec:training}
{\color{red}
The reported Fig.~\ref{fig:comm_necessity}--Fig.~\ref{fig:comm_timing} checkpoints use the same Standard reward generation. Rather than treating the reward as one long analytical expression, it is useful to view it as four groups:
\begin{equation}
R_i=R_i^{\mathrm{task}}+R_i^{\mathrm{safety}}+R_i^{\mathrm{eff}}+R_i^{\mathrm{COLREGs}}.
\label{eq:reward_groups}
\end{equation}
The task component contains arrival, time, and goal-progress terms. The safety component contains collision, proximity, collision-course, and pairwise-risk penalties. The efficiency component penalizes control effort and abrupt rudder motion while encouraging forward, goal-directed motion. The COLREGs component is a directional noncompliance penalty: Head-On, Give-Way, and Overtaking situations penalize port-side steering, whereas Stand-On penalizes unnecessary course change. This shaping is activated only for sufficiently risky encounters and uses coefficient $0.45$. It therefore acts as a common coordination convention rather than as a separate learned classifier.

The policy is optimized with PPO \citep{PPO}. The implementation uses $\gamma=0.99$, GAE parameter $0.95$, learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, clip parameter $0.2$, two PPO epochs per rollout, minibatch size 512, entropy coefficient $0.01$, value coefficient $0.5$, and gradient clipping at 0.5.

Training uses a two-stage communication schedule within one continuous 16-million-decision run. Incoming communication is zero during the first 9 million decisions so that local navigation is established first. Communication is then activated without resetting the policy parameters. The COLREGs coefficient remains unchanged across the two stages. A separate communication-from-start experiment in Section~\ref{sec:timing_result} tests whether this delayed introduction improves optimization stability.
}

% =====================================================================
\section{Simulation Environment and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:simulation}
% =====================================================================
{\color{red}
The numerical experiments are generated with the GPU-batched simulator \texttt{vessel\_gym.py}. The simulator reproduces the vessel dynamics, radar sensing, collision geometry, COLREGs situation logic, and observation contract of the Unity/C\# reference implementation while allowing many environments to run in parallel. The Unity implementation remains useful for visualization and sim-to-sim inspection, but it is not the runtime used to generate the principal numerical results.

The arena is $600\times600$~m and contains nine circular obstacles of radius 20~m arranged on a $3\times3$ grid. Each environment contains 16 vessels. A ring factor of 0.7 scales the spawn and goal geometry, producing an average direct trip of approximately 373~m. Initial headings point toward the assigned goals with a random perturbation within $\pm10^\circ$, and each vessel receives an independently sampled maximum-speed multiplier from $\mathcal{U}(0.8,1.8)$.

Radar range is 56~m, communication range is 420~m, and at most four communication partners are retained. Physics is integrated at 0.04~s with ten substeps per policy decision, giving one control decision every 0.4~s. Rudder command is bounded by $30^\circ$, the physical rudder slew rate is $12^\circ$/s, and goal arrival is declared within 3~m. The common training configuration uses 128 parallel environments, rollout length 32, and seeds 42, 43, and 44.

Primary metrics are arrival rate, collision rate, COLREGs compliance, fuel/control-effort accumulation, heading travel, minimum vessel separation, and episode length. Learning-log scores and fixed-policy evaluation metrics are not mixed in the same quantitative comparison.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
% =====================================================================
{\color{red}
The experiments are ordered as design questions rather than as a repeated list of metrics. The first six figures progressively test the communication and policy architecture. The last two figures examine robustness to heterogeneous communication and compatibility with route-level planning.
}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Is Inter-Vessel Communication Useful?}
\label{sec:comm_result}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.96\textwidth]{paper_figures/Fig1_Communication_necessity}
    \caption{Effect of inter-vessel communication under the proposed shared-perception architecture. The panels compare collision, arrival, aggregate COLREGs compliance, situation-specific compliance, and navigation-efficiency measures. Communication primarily improves safety and crossing coordination rather than merely increasing arrival rate.}
    \label{fig:comm_necessity}
\end{figure*}

{\color{red}
Fig.~\ref{fig:comm_necessity} compares the same policy architecture with communication disabled and enabled. Collision rate decreases from 2.77\% to 1.83\%, while aggregate COLREGs compliance increases from 48.4\% to 52.3\%. Arrival changes only modestly, from 54.4\% to 55.6\%, which is important because the safety improvement is not explained by a policy that simply stops attempting the task. Fuel/control-effort accumulation and heading travel also decrease, indicating that the cooperative policy avoids collision with smoother rather than more aggressive maneuvers.

The situation-specific panel shows where communication matters. Head-On compliance is already nearly saturated without communication, whereas Give-Way and Stand-On improve more strongly. Overtaking is the exception and decreases from 76\% to 70\%. The result therefore supports a narrower and more informative conclusion than ``communication improves every metric'': the channel is most useful when coordination depends on resolving interaction ambiguity that is not fully captured by local sensing.
}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Where Should Expert Specialization Occur?}
\label{sec:moe_result}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.97\textwidth]{paper_figures/Fig2_MoE_architecture}
    \caption{Comparison of four policy decompositions: a single network, a parameter-matched thin separated MoE, a full-width separated MoE, and the proposed shared-perception MoE. The figure links architectural structure to parameter count, collision rate, and arrival rate.}
    \label{fig:moe_architecture}
\end{figure*}

{\color{red}
Fig.~\ref{fig:moe_architecture} tests the central architectural claim. A single network contains 369,131 parameters. A thin separated MoE uses five narrow expert networks and is approximately parameter matched at 363,004 parameters. A full-width separated MoE duplicates the perception pipeline and grows to 1,826,719 parameters. The proposed structure shares perception but retains five full-width decision cores, requiring 511,543 parameters.

The single network reaches 57.2\% arrival but 6.07\% collision, whereas the proposed shared-perception model reaches 55.6\% arrival with only 1.83\% collision. The thin separated model performs substantially worse, showing that simply partitioning a fixed parameter budget into several complete networks fragments representational capacity. The full separated model restores capacity but at roughly 3.6 times the parameter count of the proposed model; its collision rate remains 6.40\%. These results support specialization after shared perception rather than full-network duplication. The thin condition currently has one completed seed, so it is treated as a structural comparison rather than a precise multi-seed effect-size estimate.
}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{How Many Neighbor Messages Should Be Aggregated?}
\label{sec:aggregation_result}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.96\textwidth]{paper_figures/Fig3_Multi_neighbour_aggregation}
    \caption{Nearest-one versus four-neighbor position-grounded aggregation. The schematic illustrates the information difference, and the quantitative panels report collision, situation-specific COLREGs compliance, and navigation-efficiency measures.}
    \label{fig:multi_neighbor}
\end{figure*}

{\color{red}
Fig.~\ref{fig:multi_neighbor} changes only the number of retained communication partners. Using four neighbors reduces collision from 3.43\% to 1.83\% and raises aggregate COLREGs compliance from 45.6\% to 52.3\%. Fuel/control effort and heading travel also decrease. Situation-specific compliance improves across the active encounter classes.

The result is consistent with the failure mode of pairwise communication in a multi-vessel encounter. An action that resolves the currently nearest conflict can move the own ship toward another vessel whose information has been discarded. Aggregating several spatially grounded messages allows the policy to respond to the local traffic configuration rather than repeatedly solving one pair at a time.
}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{How Wide Should the Latent Message Be?}
\label{sec:dimension_result}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.94\textwidth]{paper_figures/Fig4_Message_dimensionality}
    \caption{Sensitivity to latent message dimension. Arrival, collision, COLREGs compliance, and the outcome score in Eq.~\eqref{eq:outcome_score} are shown for message dimensions 2--12. This sweep uses the full-width separated MoE and is interpreted as a channel-capacity study rather than an ablation of the final shared-perception architecture.}
    \label{fig:message_dimension}
\end{figure*}

{\color{red}
Fig.~\ref{fig:message_dimension} sweeps message dimension over $\{2,4,6,8,10,12\}$. Very small channels degrade performance: dimensions 2 and 4 show higher collision and lower outcome score. Dimension 8 gives the highest measured outcome score, whereas dimension 12 gives the highest measured aggregate compliance. The sequence beyond the moderate-dimensional regime is not monotonic, so the data do not support a unique optimum.

The appropriate interpretation is therefore that the channel must be large enough to avoid an information bottleneck, but increasing width beyond that regime does not produce a systematic gain. Dimension 6 is retained in the common experiments because it provides a compact channel while keeping all other principal comparisons matched. The sweep was generated with the full-width separated MoE, which is explicitly disclosed to avoid presenting it as a direct shared-perception ablation.
}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{What Does COLREGs Shaping Contribute?}
\label{sec:colregs_result}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.94\textwidth]{paper_figures/Fig5_COLREGs_shaping}
    \caption{Effect of directional COLREGs shaping. The schematic shows how the shaping breaks symmetric avoidance choices, while the quantitative panels compare collision rate and COLREGs compliance with and without the rule term.}
    \label{fig:colregs_shaping}
\end{figure*}

{\color{red}
Fig.~\ref{fig:colregs_shaping} compares the proposed shared-perception policy with the COLREGs coefficient set to 0 and 0.45. In the matched three-seed comparison, the directional rule term raises aggregate compliance from approximately 31.4\% to 52.3\% and reduces collision from approximately 6.43\% to 1.83\%.

The collision reduction is as important as the compliance increase. The COLREGs term supplies a common directional convention: Head-On, Give-Way, and Overtaking discourage port turns, while Stand-On discourages unnecessary maneuvering. This breaks a symmetric failure mode in which two individually cautious agents can choose incompatible avoidance directions. The rule term therefore acts as a domain-informed coordination prior rather than merely an evaluation bonus.
}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{When Should Communication Be Introduced?}
\label{sec:timing_result}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.92\textwidth]{paper_figures/Fig6_Communication_timing}
    \caption{Communication-introduction timing. Both conditions use communication by the end of training; one activates it from the start, while the proposed schedule activates it after 9M decisions. Collision, COLREGs compliance, and arrival are reported, with the known arrival-rate seed variability shown in the final panel.}
    \label{fig:comm_timing}
\end{figure*}

{\color{red}
Fig.~\ref{fig:comm_timing} separates the value of communication from the optimization schedule used to learn it. Communication from the first decision produces 61.3\% mean arrival but 6.53\% collision and 46.4\% compliance. Delayed communication produces 55.6\% arrival, 1.83\% collision, and 52.3\% compliance. The arrival mean for communication-from-start is also highly seed sensitive, with approximately $\pm14.5$ percentage points of variation compared with $\pm1.4$ for delayed activation.

The result is therefore a safety-and-stability result, not a claim that delayed communication improves every metric. Learning a usable local navigation policy before coupling agents through a learned channel produces a more conservative and substantially more repeatable solution across seeds.
}

\subsection{Can the Local Policy Be Combined with Global A* Guidance?}
\label{sec:astar_result}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.98\textwidth]{paper_figures/Fig8_Astar_integration}
    \caption{Integration with route-level A* guidance. The existing figure connects a real-coastline A* route to a local corridor-navigation example. The corridor trajectory in the archived panel was generated with the communication-from-start checkpoint rather than the final proposed checkpoint, and the planned controlled direct-versus-A* comparison remains to be regenerated.}
    \label{fig:astar_integration}
\end{figure*}

{\color{red}
The proposed communication policy is a local controller rather than a global voyage planner. Fig.~\ref{fig:astar_integration} shows how the two levels can be connected: A* supplies a sequence of collision-free route-level waypoints, and the learned policy treats the current waypoint as its local goal while resolving dynamic vessel interactions.

The route generator has been checked over all 320 combinations of 20 spawn points and 16 goals in the controlled map. It must also use the proposed shared-perception checkpoint with ring factor 0.7. The archived corridor panel in Fig.~\ref{fig:astar_integration} uses the weaker communication-from-start checkpoint and is retained only to show the integration mechanism. Until the controlled rerun is complete, the paper claims compatibility with an external global planner, not a demonstrated A*-induced navigation-performance gain.
}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Overall Interpretation}
{\color{red}
Taken together, Figs.~\ref{fig:comm_necessity}--\ref{fig:comm_timing} support a coherent algorithmic interpretation. Communication addresses missing local information; spatial grounding prevents message identity from being lost during multi-neighbor aggregation; shared perception avoids duplicating the dominant representation cost across COLREGs situations; situation-specific decision cores preserve behaviorally different obligations; directional COLREGs shaping supplies a common maneuver convention; and delayed channel activation reduces the optimization instability of learning navigation and communication simultaneously.

This interpretation is stronger than selecting the best number in each ablation independently. The method is a decomposition of the cooperative-navigation problem according to which information should be shared, specialized, or communicated. The final two studies extend that decomposition toward deployment: Fig.~\ref{fig:heterogeneous_fleet} asks whether cooperation degrades gracefully when communication capability is mixed, and Fig.~\ref{fig:astar_integration} asks whether the local learned controller can sit underneath a conventional route planner.
}

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================
{\color{red}
This paper presented a communication-enabled cooperative navigation framework for MASS under partial observability. The framework is organized around one design principle: share common perception, specialize situation-dependent decision making, and communicate information that is locally missing. A shared radar encoder extracts a common physical representation of the surrounding scene, deterministic COLREGs geometry routes each sample to a situation-specific decision core, and compact learned messages extend the receiving vessel's information beyond its own radar horizon. Relative geometry is attached at the receiver before multi-neighbor aggregation, separating message semantics from spatial attribution.

The controlled experiments show that these choices address distinct failure modes. Communication lowers collision rate and particularly improves crossing coordination. Aggregating several neighbors is safer than retaining only the nearest vessel. Sharing perception provides a substantially better safety--parameter tradeoff than either a single network or full expert duplication. Directional COLREGs shaping functions as a coordination prior, and introducing communication after local navigation has stabilized yields much lower collision and lower seed sensitivity than activating communication from the first training step.

The current study abstracts the physical communication channel as range-limited message exchange. The heterogeneous-fleet and A* experiments also contain identified provenance or evaluation tasks that are explicitly separated from the finalized primary results. Future work will resolve these remaining evaluations, incorporate latency, packet loss, and bandwidth constraints, train directly with route-level waypoint sequences, and test the learned controller in higher-fidelity maritime traffic environments.
}
